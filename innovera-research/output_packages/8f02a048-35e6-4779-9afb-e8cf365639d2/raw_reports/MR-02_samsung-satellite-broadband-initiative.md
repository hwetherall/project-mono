Title: Serviceable Addressable Market, Serviceable Obtainable Market, and Market Reachability for LEO Satellite Broadband (Samsung Venture Brief)

Date: March 20, 2026

Executive summary

- The LEO broadband category is scaling quickly from both a revenue and subscriber perspective, and competition beyond the U.S. is accelerating. Deloitte forecasts around US$15 billion in LEO broadband revenues in 2026, with global subscribers surpassing 15 million by year-end, driven increasingly by emerging markets where terrestrial alternatives are limited and governments emphasize resilience and sovereignty ([Deloitte, 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/technology/technology-media-and-telecom-predictions/2026/next-gen-satellite-internet.html)).

- Using ex‑U.S. share proxies from global performance samples (Starlink accounts for 97.1% of satellite Speedtest samples globally; the U.S. accounts for only 22.5% of those samples), the 2026 ex‑U.S. LEO SAM is approximately US$11.6–12.0 billion, scaling toward US$18–24.6 billion by 2030 for the broader satellite internet market (LEO + GEO/MEO) per multiple market trackers; LEO’s share continues to rise as a percentage of that total in developing regions ([PCMag, 2026](https://www.pcmag.com/news/in-the-satellite-internet-race-starlink-crushes-rivals-with-97-of-speed); [Telecompetitor, 2026](https://www.telecompetitor.com/starlink-median-u-s-speeds-still-short-of-federal-minimums-ookla-report/); [Deloitte, 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/technology/technology-media-and-telecom-predictions/2026/next-gen-satellite-internet.html); [Research and Markets via Yahoo, 2026](https://uk.finance.yahoo.com/news/satellite-internet-market-intelligence-report-164800004.html); [MarketsandMarkets, 2026](https://www.marketsandmarkets.com/Market-Reports/satellite-internet-market-139239513.html)).

- Routes to market are bifurcating: direct-to-consumer (D2C) dominates consumer broadband where allowed (Starlink’s D2C scale reflects this), while enterprise, government, and mobility segments are primarily addressed via telco channels, resellers, and multi-orbit service integrators. Deloitte expects both cooperation (with telcos) and competition (D2C) strategies to intensify in 2026 and beyond ([Deloitte, 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/technology/technology-media-and-telecom-predictions/2026/next-gen-satellite-internet.html); [Via Satellite, 2026](https://www.satellitetoday.com/connectivity/2026/02/06/data-shows-starlink-dominates-consumer-internet-market-at-least-for-now-ookla-analyst-says/)).

- Access gates are real and vary by jurisdiction. Southeast Asia is opening (e.g., Vietnam approved Starlink with plans for four gateways to serve up to 600,000 users during an initial pilot), while the EU is formalizing sovereign connectivity (GovSatCom live in 2026; IRIS² advancing), and U.S. national security/foreign ownership regimes are tightening—increasing the potential for sovereign-neutral alternatives to gain preference in select regions ([SAMENA/DevelopingTelecoms, 2026](https://www.samenacouncil.org/samena_daily_news?news=109546); [EC Luxembourg Representation, 2026](https://luxembourg.representation.ec.europa.eu/actualites-et-evenements/actualites/eu-advances-secure-satellite-communications-govsatcom-and-iris2-2026-01-27_fr?prefLang=en); [Mayer Brown, 2026](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/02/fccs-january-actions-intensify-national-security-and-foreign-ownership-oversight)).

- A Samsung-led constellation could credibly reach multi‑billion scale ex‑U.S. within a decade if it prioritizes: (1) a sovereignty‑aligned distribution model with regional partners and co‑investment (especially in Southeast Asia and the Middle East), (2) differentiated value where U.S.-aligned operators face regulatory friction (or sovereign concerns), (3) tight hardware–network–cloud integration (terminals, chipsets, and edge platforms), and (4) staged constellation deployment to match market-access milestones and capex pacing. Amazon’s 2025–2026 launch playbook (80+ launches procured across multiple providers) demonstrates that scale is feasible without owning a launcher, though launch vendor diversity is essential for resilience ([About Amazon, 2026](https://www.aboutamazon.com/what-we-do/devices-services/amazon-leo)).

- Recommendation: Proceed to a de-risked Phase 1 with a regional-first constellation and go-to-market plan in Southeast Asia and the Middle East, backed by anchor partnerships (MNOs/ISPs/SWFs), spectrum access agreements, and sovereign-compliance certifications. Lock in multi-orbit integration and wholesale channels for enterprise/government. Avoid a global “all-in” megaconstellation until regulatory and channel access are secured. A 3,000–3,500 satellite design, phased by region, is credible for coverage and performance for initial markets; replacement needs (20–25% annually due to 4–5-year LEO satellite life) must be baked into the long-term capex model ([Deloitte, 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/technology/technology-media-and-telecom-predictions/2026/next-gen-satellite-internet.html)).

1. Market context and opportunity

1.1 Category momentum and competitive baseline

- LEO broadband continues rapid expansion. Deloitte projects around US$15 billion in LEO revenues and 15+ million global LEO broadband subscribers in 2026, reflecting growing deployment and uptake across consumer and enterprise segments ([Deloitte, 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/technology/technology-media-and-telecom-predictions/2026/next-gen-satellite-internet.html)).

- Amazon rebranded Project Kuiper to Amazon Leo in November 2025 and began full-scale deployment in April 2025, targeting a >3,000 satellite constellation with optical inter-satellite links and three antennas (Nano/Pro/Ultra), with pilots and wholesale agreements announced (e.g., NBN Co in Australia; Connected Farms), and claims of >1 Gbps endpoint speeds on Leo Ultra ([Aviation Week, 2025](https://aviationweek.com/space/satellites/amazon-rebrands-project-kuiper-amazon-leo); [About Amazon, 2026](https://www.aboutamazon.com/what-we-do/devices-services/amazon-leo)).

- Starlink remains the dominant incumbent on consumer performance and scale—97.1% of all global satellite Speedtest samples in Q3 2025 came from Starlink users; the U.S. accounted for 22.5% of Speedtest samples, with the remainder ex‑U.S. This reflects both Starlink’s D2C model and its global footprint; however, capacity constraints periodically limit signups in major African cities, suggesting both demand and network planning challenges at scale ([PCMag, 2026](https://www.pcmag.com/news/in-the-satellite-internet-race-starlink-crushes-rivals-with-97-of-speed); [Ookla Africa, 2025](https://www.ookla.com/articles/starlink-ssa-q1-2025)).

- GEO incumbents are refocusing on wholesale/mobility/government as LEO competition intensifies in consumer broadband. Via Satellite notes Viasat and HughesNet have seen consumer declines and are emphasizing wholesale and mobility, while Starlink increasingly competes in those segments as well ([Via Satellite, 2026](https://www.satellitetoday.com/connectivity/2026/02/06/data-shows-starlink-dominates-consumer-internet-market-at-least-for-now-ookla-analyst-says/)).

- Multiorbit integration is accelerating: Intelsat committed US$250 million over six years (with an option for another US$250 million) for Eutelsat OneWeb LEO services to bolster a multi-orbit strategy—an indicator of enterprise/government demand for managed GEO/LEO continuity and sovereign-grade reliability ([Intelsat, 2024](https://www.intelsat.com/newsroom/intelsat-advances-multi-orbit-strategy-with-expansion-of-eutelsat-group-leo-agreement/)).

- Regional policies and sovereignty initiatives are reshaping competitive dynamics. The EU launched GovSatCom operations in 2026 and is advancing IRIS² to secure sovereign, trusted satellite connectivity; draft cybersecurity frameworks (CSA 2) further harden supply chain requirements. These factors could increase barriers to U.S.-aligned operators in sensitive contracts, and re-weight demand toward neutral or regional providers ([EC Luxembourg Representation, 2026](https://luxembourg.representation.ec.europa.eu/actualites-et-evenements/actualites/eu-advances-secure-satellite-communications-govsatcom-and-iris2-2026-01-27_fr?prefLang=en); [Taylor Wessing, 2026](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2026/01/telecommunications-law-and-regulation-2026)).

1.2 Serviceable Addressable Market (SAM) and Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM)

Definitions and approach

- SAM (serviceable addressable market): The portion of total global satellite broadband demand that a non‑U.S.‑aligned LEO provider can address, given geography (Southeast Asia, Middle East, global ex‑U.S.), product scope (consumer + enterprise/government connectivity with hardware + subscription), and route‑to‑market constraints.

- SOM (serviceable obtainable market): The realistic share of SAM attainable by a new entrant over a planning horizon (10 years), given competitive incumbency, regulatory gates, channel access, launch cadence, and capex constraints.

Sizing inputs

- Deloitte (2026) forecast: ~US$15B LEO revenue; >15M subscribers by end‑2026 (global) ([Deloitte, 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/technology/technology-media-and-telecom-predictions/2026/next-gen-satellite-internet.html)).

- Satellite internet total market 2030 forecasts (all orbits):
  - Research & Markets (R&M): US$24.6B by 2030 (from US$5.1B in 2024) ([Research & Markets via Yahoo, 2026](https://uk.finance.yahoo.com/news/satellite-internet-market-intelligence-report-164800004.html)).
  - MarketsandMarkets: US$33.44B by 2030 (from US$14.56B in 2025) ([MarketsandMarkets, 2026](https://www.marketsandmarkets.com/Market-Reports/satellite-internet-market-139239513.html)).
  - Stratistics MRC (broadband services): US$17.92B by 2034; US$7.03B in 2026 baseline ([Stratistics, 2026](https://www.marketresearch.com/Stratistics-Market-Research-Consulting-v4058/Satellite-Broadband-Services-Forecasts-Global-44083244/)).

- Ex‑U.S. share proxy: Starlink Speedtest sample distribution suggests U.S. ~22.5%; ex‑U.S. ~77.5% of global satellite Speedtest traffic, a reasonable first-order proxy for ex‑U.S. demand weighting (caveat: not a direct revenue share) ([Telecompetitor, 2026](https://www.telecompetitor.com/starlink-median-u-s-speeds-still-short-of-federal-minimums-ookla-report/)).

- Regional growth drivers: Emerging markets (e.g., India, Indonesia) expected to drive growth; distribution increasingly via telcos and CSPs in new markets ([Juniper Research press, 2025](https://www.juniperresearch.com/press/satellite-broadband-market-to-break-20bn/)).

- Country‑level access examples: Vietnam licensed Starlink for fixed and mobile services, with four planned gateways and capacity up to 600,000 users in the first phase, indicating addressable household potential in a single Southeast Asian market once regulatory access is granted ([SAMENA/DevelopingTelecoms, 2026](https://www.samenacouncil.org/samena_daily_news?news=109546)).

SAM estimation (ex‑U.S.)

- 2026 ex‑U.S. LEO SAM (revenue): Applying the ~77.5% ex‑U.S. share proxy to Deloitte’s 2026 LEO revenue estimate (~US$15B) yields ~US$11.6B ex‑U.S. SAM for 2026 (sensitivity: US$10.5–12.5B). This captures the serviceable revenue opportunity addressable by a non‑U.S. provider outside the U.S., assuming comparable ARPU and segment mix ([Deloitte, 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/technology/technology-media-and-telecom-predictions/2026/next-gen-satellite-internet.html); [Telecompetitor, 2026](https://www.telecompetitor.com/starlink-median-u-s-speeds-still-short-of-federal-minimums-ookla-report/)).

- 2030 ex‑U.S. Satellite Internet SAM (all orbits): Applying the same ex‑U.S. share to 2030 forecasts yields:
  - R&M: US$24.6B × 77.5% ≈ US$19.1B ex‑U.S. SAM (all orbits, 2030) ([Research & Markets via Yahoo, 2026](https://uk.finance.yahoo.com/news/satellite-internet-market-intelligence-report-164800004.html)).
  - MarketsandMarkets: US$33.44B × 77.5% ≈ US$25.9B ex‑U.S. SAM (all orbits, 2030) ([MarketsandMarkets, 2026](https://www.marketsandmarkets.com/Market-Reports/satellite-internet-market-139239513.html)).

Subscriber SAM benchmarks

- Deloitte’s 2026 subscribers: 15M global; ex‑U.S. proxy at 77.5% suggests ~11.6–12.0M ex‑U.S. LEO subscribers in 2026 addressable by providers with market access ([Deloitte, 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/technology/technology-media-and-telecom-predictions/2026/next-gen-satellite-internet.html); [Telecompetitor, 2026](https://www.telecompetitor.com/starlink-median-u-s-speeds-still-short-of-federal-minimums-ookla-report/)).

- MarketsandMarkets (consumer broadband LEO subscribers): 6.2M in 2025 growing to 15.6M by 2030 (category-level; not region-specific) ([MarketsandMarkets, 2026](https://www.marketsandmarkets.com/Market-Reports/satellite-internet-market-139239513.html)).

SOM framing (Samsung potential)

- Attainable SOM depends on country-by-country licensing, telco/SWF partnerships, and constellation staging. As an analog, Amazon Leo’s public anchor agreements and 80+ launch procurements show credible scale-up without a proprietary launcher, but with heavy multi‑vendor launch reliance and multiple years of precommercial deployment ([About Amazon, 2026](https://www.aboutamazon.com/what-we-do/devices-services/amazon-leo)).

- A phased ex‑U.S. SOM target could be 5–10% of ex‑U.S. LEO revenue by Year 10 (i.e., ~US$1.0–2.5B annualized by 2035 if the ex‑U.S. SAM is ~US$20–26B by 2030 and continues to grow), contingent on:
  - Priority access in 3–5 anchor countries (e.g., SEA/ME); 
  - Competitive terminal pricing and wholesale plans via MNOs/ISPs;
  - Regulatory acceptance as a sovereign-neutral provider;
  - Early-mover differentiation in direct-to-cell, multi-orbit interoperability, and enterprise SLAs.
  
This SOM is an inference based on comparable category diffusion and channel-led distribution gains in emerging markets; robust data for Samsung-specific share is not available and would require further primary validation.

1.3 Where demand is strongest outside the U.S.

- Emerging markets are accelerating adoption: Juniper forecasts satellite broadband growth driven by India and Indonesia over the next five years and advises providers to partner with MNOs/ISPs for reselling in these markets ([Juniper Research press, 2025](https://www.juniperresearch.com/press/satellite-broadband-market-to-break-20bn/)).

- Southeast Asia is opening to LEO: Vietnam’s 2026 approval for Starlink (fixed+mobile satellite) with four planned gateways and up to 600,000 users initial capacity is a tangible data point for serviceable demand in one country. Regulatory pilots allow operations through 2030 under relaxed foreign ownership constraints, signaling a positive policy stance toward LEO entrants ([SAMENA/DevelopingTelecoms, 2026](https://www.samenacouncil.org/samena_daily_news?news=109546)).

- Policy and sovereignty in Europe: EU GovSatCom operations commenced in 2026; IRIS² is advancing. Security frameworks (e.g., CSA 2 draft) and “sovereign cloud” procurement indicate a preference for EU-controlled infrastructure for sensitive workloads, which may extend to satellite services in government/critical sectors ([EC Luxembourg Representation, 2026](https://luxembourg.representation.ec.europa.eu/actualites-et-evenements/actualites/eu-advances-secure-satellite-communications-govsatcom-and-iris2-2026-01-27_fr?prefLang=en); [Interoperable Europe, 2026](https://interoperable-europe.ec.europa.eu/collection/eprocurement/news/cloud-sovereignty-framework)).

2. A. Route-to-market and channel structure

2.1 Dominant route-to-market types

- Direct-to-consumer (D2C) self-serve: Starlink’s global consumer leadership and 97.1% share of satellite Speedtest samples demonstrates the scale of the D2C channel wherever regulators allow direct retail and equipment shipments; plan/pricing tiers (e.g., $50–$120/month, various hardware SKUs) reflect consumer-grade self-serve GTM with optional rentals/installment programs in price-sensitive markets ([PCMag, 2026](https://www.pcmag.com/news/in-the-satellite-internet-race-starlink-crushes-rivals-with-97-of-speed); [PCMag pricing test, 2026](https://me.pcmag.com/en/networking/36066/is-starlinks-80-monthly-lite-plan-fast-enough-i-tested-it-out); [Dawan Africa, 2026](https://dawan.africa/news/starlink-cuts-upfront-cost-with-new-instalment-payment-plan-to-attract-kenyan-users)).

- Telco/MNO and ISP partners (wholesale/resale/backhaul): Deloitte identifies cooperation vs. competition as diverging strategies. Partnerships with local telcos are widespread in LEO broadband and D2D (direct‑to‑device) in Japan, Australia, the Philippines; they are “capex‑lite” ways for telcos to meet universal service pressure. Examples: Amazon Leo’s NBN Co wholesale agreement in Australia; Connected Farms pilots; Airtel Africa’s integration deal with Starlink for backhaul/coverage in nine of 14 countries ([Deloitte, 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/technology/technology-media-and-telecom-predictions/2026/next-gen-satellite-internet.html); [Aviation Week, 2025](https://aviationweek.com/space/satellites/amazon-rebrands-project-kuiper-amazon-leo); [Ookla Africa, 2025](https://www.ookla.com/articles/starlink-ssa-q1-2025)).

- B2B/government via multi-orbit service integrators and satellite operators: Incumbents like Intelsat, SES, and Eutelsat OneWeb package GEO+LEO services with SLAs for enterprise, aviation, maritime, and government. Intelsat’s US$250M OneWeb LEO commitment illustrates an RTM path where LEO capacity is embedded in existing B2B/Gov channels and contracts ([Intelsat, 2024](https://www.intelsat.com/newsroom/intelsat-advances-multi-orbit-strategy-with-expansion-of-eutelsat-group-leo-agreement/)).

- Regional distributors/resellers: OneWeb’s distribution partnerships (e.g., RLTT) indicate strong reliance on local resellers for market access and service delivery in government/enterprise; this pattern is common for sovereign-sensitive or regulated verticals ([Space in Africa, 2023](https://spaceinafrica.com/2023/12/15/eutelsat-oneweb-signs-a-multi-million-distribution-partnership-agreement-with-rltt/)).

Why these routes dominate

- Consumer friction is minimized via D2C when national approvals allow import/installation. Where D2C is limited or prohibited, wholesale/resale ties to licensed telecom operators provide essential “landing rights,” spectrum coordination, billing, and field support, while satisfying universal service mandates without telco capex ([Deloitte, 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/technology/technology-media-and-telecom-predictions/2026/next-gen-satellite-internet.html)).

- Enterprise/government buyers require managed service SLAs, network integration, and compliance (e.g., lawful intercept, data sovereignty), which are best addressed by integrators or existing satellite operators with multi-orbit portfolios and in-country operations ([Intelsat, 2024](https://www.intelsat.com/newsroom/intelsat-advances-multi-orbit-strategy-with-expansion-of-eutelsat-group-leo-agreement/)).

2.2 Channel share and segment coverage (quantified where possible)

- Consumer satellite broadband usage is overwhelmingly Starlink (97.1% of satellite Speedtest tests, Q3 2025), implying heavy D2C share of consumer channel in markets where it is permitted. In Nigeria, Starlink became the second-largest ISP with 65,564 subscribers by Q3 2024; in Kenya, satellite subs more than doubled in Q3 2024. These are early proof points of D2C uptake in emerging markets when available ([PCMag, 2026](https://www.pcmag.com/news/in-the-satellite-internet-race-starlink-crushes-rivals-with-97-of-speed); [Ookla Africa, 2025](https://www.ookla.com/articles/starlink-ssa-q1-2025)).

- B2B/government segments increasingly consume via wholesale and integrators. Viasat and HughesNet are pivoting to wholesale customers (governments, airlines, maritime), while Intelsat’s $250M LEO commitment to OneWeb is a scale signal for LEO capacity in B2B routes. This suggests B2B/government channel share is dominated by telco/ISP resellers and multi-orbit operators, though precise percentages are not publicly broken out in provided sources ([Via Satellite, 2026](https://www.satellitetoday.com/connectivity/2026/02/06/data-shows-starlink-dominates-consumer-internet-market-at-least-for-now-ookla-analyst-says/); [Intelsat, 2024](https://www.intelsat.com/newsroom/intelsat-advances-multi-orbit-strategy-with-expansion-of-eutelsat-group-leo-agreement/)).

- D2D (direct‑to‑device) will be driven through MNOs. Deloitte highlights telco partnerships for D2D in Japan, Australia, and the Philippines; AST SpaceMobile expects initial beta with AT&T in H1 2026, underscoring MNO-led distribution for handset connectivity ([Deloitte, 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/technology/technology-media-and-telecom-predictions/2026/next-gen-satellite-internet.html); [Via Satellite, 2026](https://www.satellitetoday.com/opinion/2026/01/16/convergence-comes-of-age-2026-shifts-satellite-promise-into-commercial-reality/)).

2.3 Marketplace or platform access requirements

- Telecom channels and government procurement impose compliance gates (see Section B). For EU sovereign workloads, alignment with EU cybersecurity and sovereignty frameworks (e.g., GovSatCom/IRIS² and evolving CSA 2 requirements) is increasingly important for listing/eligibility. For the U.S. (if targeting multinational customers with U.S. ops), expanding FCC national security reporting and foreign ownership transparency regimes now impose annual certifications and potential Team Telecom referrals—factors that U.S.-aligned operators must satisfy and that may impact partner decisions in sensitive sectors ([EC Luxembourg Representation, 2026](https://luxembourg.representation.ec.europa.eu/actualites-et-evenements/actualites/eu-advances-secure-satellite-communications-govsatcom-and-iris2-2026-01-27_fr?prefLang=en); [Mayer Brown, 2026](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/02/fccs-january-actions-intensify-national-security-and-foreign-ownership-oversight)).

- Public cloud and digital marketplaces are relevant for enterprise integration (e.g., AWS Ground Station tie-ins noted for Amazon Leo). However, formal “listing” requirements in hyperscaler marketplaces are not specified in the provided sources; integration standards, data residency, and security attestations would be expected for enterprise distribution but require case-by-case validation ([About Amazon, 2026](https://www.aboutamazon.com/what-we-do/devices-services/amazon-leo)).

3. B. Access and compliance gates

3.4 Jurisdiction-specific licensing and compliance requirements

- Southeast Asia (example—Vietnam): Vietnam approved Starlink’s fixed and mobile satellite services in 2026, granting spectrum/radio equipment permissions and allowing four gateway stations targeting up to 600,000 users. The approval follows a 2025 policy establishing a 5‑year pilot enabling LEO providers to operate without foreign ownership limits, with the pilot running through end‑2030. This is a template for what a favorable SEA regime can look like (structured pilots; gateway and spectrum authorization; local compliance) ([SAMENA/DevelopingTelecoms, 2026](https://www.samenacouncil.org/samena_daily_news?news=109546)).

- European Union (ex‑U.S., sovereign sensitivity): GovSatCom operations kicked off in 2026, with IRIS² offering secure communications expansion by 2027 via strictly controlled commercial partners. Parallel policy moves (draft CSA 2) tighten ICT supply chain requirements, mandating replacement of certain high‑risk supplier components and strengthening certification frameworks. EU initiatives support procurement preferences for sovereign/trusted operators—material for distribution in government/defense across ex‑U.S. geographies ([EC Luxembourg Representation, 2026](https://luxembourg.representation.ec.europa.eu/actualites-et-evenements/actualites/eu-advances-secure-satellite-communications-govsatcom-and-iris2-2026-01-27_fr?prefLang=en); [Taylor Wessing, 2026](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2026/01/telecommunications-law-and-regulation-2026)).

- U.S. national security rules (context for U.S.-aligned competitors operating globally): The FCC in early 2026 intensified national security and foreign adversary ownership reporting, proposing annual certifications for “Covered Authorization” holders across wireless, satellite, media, submarine cable, etc., and streamlined revocation paths for noncompliance. These mechanisms, plus Team Telecom oversight, can affect how U.S.-aligned providers manage global partnerships and may be perceived by third countries in sovereignty risk assessments ([Mayer Brown, 2026](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/02/fccs-january-actions-intensify-national-security-and-foreign-ownership-oversight); [Pillsbury, 2026](https://www.pillsburylaw.com/en/news-and-insights/fcc-enforcement-action-violating-team-telecom-committments.html)).

- Reciprocity and market access concerns: The FCC has asked for comment on satellite market access reciprocity in light of rising barriers faced by U.S.-flagged systems abroad (e.g., EU proposals potentially imposing requirements on non‑EU operators, local presence/fees differentials). This indicates the global environment is shifting toward reciprocal or preferential access regimes dependent on sovereignty alignment—relevant to a Samsung non‑U.S.‑aligned entrant seeking favored access in some jurisdictions ([Greenberg Traurig, 2026](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2026/3/fcc-seeks-comment-on-satellite-market-access-reciprocity)).

- Licensing mechanics and streamlining: The FCC continues to streamline licensing and renewals (e.g., 30‑day shot clock for earth station renewals; baseline earth-station licensing for Ground‑Station‑as‑a‑Service without fixed PoCs), and uses Special Temporary Authority (STA) for time‑bound operations (e.g., 30‑day TT&C STAs for ViaSat‑3 F2). While this is a U.S. example, it illustrates the tempo and documentation required in mature regulatory environments; similar timelines may or may not be matched in SEA/ME and should be planned for with local counsel ([FCC Fact Sheet, 2025](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-419794A1.pdf); [FCC STA Public Notice, 2026](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-419794A1.pdf)).

3.5 Time and cost to satisfy access requirements

- Hard public fee schedules and timelines for SEA/ME satellite broadband market access were not provided in the sources; however:
  - Vietnam’s pilot policy signals multi‑year windows (through 2030) with discrete spectrum and gateway approvals—operators should budget for gateway buildout, license fees, and testing before commercial launch (inference based on Vietnam’s gateway and capacity plan) ([SAMENA/DevelopingTelecoms, 2026](https://www.samenacouncil.org/samena_daily_news?news=109546)).
  - EU sovereign programs (GovSatCom/IRIS²) will require meeting strict security and certification controls; vendors should expect multi‑month to multi‑year certification/onboarding cycles aligned to national security vetting (inference; policy statements confirm strict security posture) ([EC Luxembourg Representation, 2026](https://luxembourg.representation.ec.europa.eu/actualites-et-evenements/actualites/eu-advances-secure-satellite-communications-govsatcom-and-iris2-2026-01-27_fr?prefLang=en)).
  - U.S. process examples—30‑day renewal shot clocks and 30‑day STAs—illustrate regulatory processing tempo for narrower authorizations; full NGSO constellations require extensive filings and ITU coordination beyond these examples (inference) ([FCC STA Public Notice, 2026](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-419794A1.pdf)).

3.6 Reachable ICP counts by subsegment (public counts from sources)

- Residential (Vietnam example): Up to 600,000 users capacity in initial phase with four gateways signals a reachable residential ICP magnitude in a single SEA country, pending device affordability and local marketing ([SAMENA/DevelopingTelecoms, 2026](https://www.samenacouncil.org/samena_daily_news?news=109546)).

- Households globally needing LEO: Amazon’s CEO publicly cited approximately 500 million households and millions of enterprises/organizations as the addressable “need” for Leo—this is a global statement of need rather than legal addressability, but frames the long-run TAM for broadband from space ([Aviation Week, 2025](https://aviationweek.com/space/satellites/amazon-rebrands-project-kuiper-amazon-leo)).

- Enterprise/government anchor channels: Intelsat’s US$250M LEO purchase signals substantial enterprise demand via multi‑orbit integrators; EU GovSatCom covers all EU member state institutions for secure comms (ICP count: 27 EU member states; multiple agencies per state). 6. Venture brief answers with evidence

Q1. Should Samsung proceed with launching a satellite-based broadband communications business similar to Starlink?

- Yes—with a region-first, sovereignty-aligned strategy focused on Southeast Asia and the Middle East, not a single global D2C push at the outset. Growth forecasts (US$15B LEO revenue and 15M+ subscribers in 2026) and opening regulatory environments (e.g., Vietnam) support a multi‑billion ex‑U.S. opportunity if regulatory access and telco partnerships are secured. EU sovereign initiatives further signal preference for non‑U.S.-controlled infrastructure in sensitive segments; similar preferences may emerge in parts of the Middle East and Asia, creating white spaces for a neutral Asian-aligned provider ([Deloitte, 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/technology/technology-media-and-telecom-predictions/2026/next-gen-satellite-internet.html); [SAMENA/DevelopingTelecoms, 2026](https://www.samenacouncil.org/samena_daily_news?news=109546); [EC Luxembourg Representation, 2026](https://luxembourg.representation.ec.europa.eu/actualites-et-evenements/actualites/eu-advances-secure-satellite-communications-govsatcom-and-iris2-2026-01-27_fr?prefLang=en)).

Q2. Can a Samsung-led service achieve the scale and economics for a multi‑tens‑of‑billions investment?

- Potentially—but only with staged deployment matched to regulatory milestones and anchor demand. At ~US$11.6B ex‑U.S. LEO SAM in 2026 (growing toward US$19–26B all‑orbit SAM by 2030), a 5–10% ex‑U.S. share by Year 10 implies US$1–2.5B annual revenue potential; a path to >US$10B annually would require broader geographic expansion and/or significant B2B/mobility penetration. Margins at maturity will depend on terminal BoM, launch pricing, satellite refresh (20–25% annually), gateway footprint, and ARPU mix. A multi-orbit and multi‑segment mix (consumer + enterprise/government + mobility) increases revenue resilience but adds capex and GTM complexity ([Deloitte, 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/technology/technology-media-and-telecom-predictions/2026/next-gen-satellite-internet.html); [MarketsandMarkets, 2026](https://www.marketsandmarkets.com/Market-Reports/satellite-internet-market-139239513.html); [Research & Markets via Yahoo, 2026](https://uk.finance.yahoo.com/news/satellite-internet-market-intelligence-report-164800004.html)).

Q3. Is it feasible to build and operate without owning a proprietary launcher?

- Yes. Amazon Leo began full‑scale deployment in April 2025 using 80+ launches contracted across Arianespace, Blue Origin, SpaceX, and ULA—the largest commercial launch procurement in history. This validates that scale can be achieved without an in‑house launch vehicle, provided vendor diversification and long‑term launch reservations are locked in for cost and schedule resilience ([About Amazon, 2026](https://www.aboutamazon.com/what-we-do/devices-services/amazon-leo)).

Q4. Are there sufficient regulatory/geopolitical white spaces favoring a non‑U.S.-aligned provider?

- Yes, in select regions and segments. EU sovereign programs and cybersecurity regimes (GovSatCom/IRIS²/CSA 2) explicitly prioritize European-controlled infrastructure; FCC commentary about reciprocity concerns highlights a global drift toward sovereignty‑based access controls. In SEA, countries like Vietnam are open but operate structured pilots; in the Middle East, existing regional operators (Yahsat/Thuraya) indicate demand for regionally controlled services and may welcome a neutral multi‑orbit partner. Samsung can position as sovereign‑neutral with regional control planes, data localization, and local gateway investments ([EC Luxembourg Representation, 2026](https://luxembourg.representation.ec.europa.eu/actualites-et-evenements/actualites/eu-advances-secure-satellite-communications-govsatcom-and-iris2-2026-01-27_fr?prefLang=en); [Greenberg Traurig, 2026](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2026/3/fcc-seeks-comment-on-satellite-market-access-reciprocity)).

Q5. What should be the beachhead market?

- Southeast Asia: Vietnam is already licensing LEO services with clear gateway plans and an initial capacity target of 600,000 users—an attractive pilot/b beachhead environment. Neighboring markets (Indonesia, Philippines, Malaysia) show telco partnership appetite for LEO/D2D distribution (per Deloitte), supporting a cluster strategy. A secondary beachhead should be a Gulf market (e.g., UAE or Saudi Arabia) where enterprise/government demand for resilient connectivity is high and regional operators may co‑invest or partner ([SAMENA/DevelopingTelecoms, 2026](https://www.samenacouncil.org/samena_daily_news?news=109546); [Deloitte, 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/technology/technology-media-and-telecom-predictions/2026/next-gen-satellite-internet.html)).

Q6. Optimal constellation size and regional deployment strategy

- A 3,000–3,500 satellite target is credible for initial regional coverage and performance—consistent with Amazon Leo’s 3,000+ design and FCC approval for 3,236. Staged deployment (e.g., 600–1,000 satellites for SEA/ME and adjacent longitudes first) can align capex to market access, with expansions as country licenses and distribution mature. Plan for 20–25% annual replacement due to 4–5‑year LEO satellite life; cash-flow models must explicitly capture this refresh cadence ([About Amazon, 2026](https://www.aboutamazon.com/what-we-do/devices-services/amazon-leo); [Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_Leo); [Deloitte, 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/technology/technology-media-and-telecom-predictions/2026/next-gen-satellite-internet.html)).

Q7. Which geographies are most credible beachheads where Samsung could win against incumbents?

- Vietnam (explicit gateway plan and capacity target), Indonesia and the Philippines (evidence of telco‑LEO cooperation elsewhere; high rural demand), and one GCC hub (UAE or Saudi Arabia) for enterprise/government corridors and maritime/aviation adjacency—paired with partnerships to access spectrum and government channels ([SAMENA/DevelopingTelecoms, 2026](https://www.samenacouncil.org/samena_daily_news?news=109546); [Deloitte, 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/technology/technology-media-and-telecom-predictions/2026/next-gen-satellite-internet.html)).

Q8. Where must Samsung be vertically integrated vs. where to partner?

- Integrate:
  - User terminals (flat-panel phased arrays), CPE chipsets, and firmware—core to cost, performance, and differentiation.
  - Network control and security stack, with options for localized control planes and data sovereignty features.
  - Cloud/edge integration to enable enterprise workloads, caching, and traffic engineering (Samsung’s device and network portfolio is an advantage).
- Partner:
  - Launch (multi‑vendor, long-term procurement as Amazon did); ground stations in-country; MNO/ISP reseller channels; multi‑orbit integration via operators like Intelsat/SES/Eutelsat OneWeb for enterprise/government SLAs ([About Amazon, 2026](https://www.aboutamazon.com/what-we-do/devices-services/amazon-leo); [Intelsat, 2024](https://www.intelsat.com/newsroom/intelsat-advances-multi-orbit-strategy-with-expansion-of-eutelsat-group-leo-agreement/)).

Q9. Optimal pace of market entry and constellation deployment

- Phase 0–1 (Years 1–3): Secure 2–3 anchor markets (e.g., Vietnam + 1 GCC + 1 additional SEA market), co‑develop gateways, and sign telco/ISP/SWF anchors before committing to mass satellite production. Target 600–1,000 satellites with regional focus.
- Phase 2 (Years 3–6): Expand to 6–10 countries; add mobility SKUs and enterprise SLAs via multi‑orbit partners; begin D2D pilots via MNOs.
- Phase 3 (Years 6–10): Scale to 3,000–3,500 satellites with broader ex‑U.S. coverage; optimize ARPU and margins via segment mix and hardware cost-downs. This pacing aligns capex with learning cycles and regulatory outcomes (inference based on Amazon’s staged approach and regulatory patterns) ([About Amazon, 2026](https://www.aboutamazon.com/what-we-do/devices-services/amazon-leo); [SAMENA/DevelopingTelecoms, 2026](https://www.samenacouncil.org/samena_daily_news?news=109546)).

7. Risks and mitigations

- Regulatory unpredictability: Country pilots (e.g., Vietnam) can be reversed or tightened; reciprocity regimes may restrict non‑aligned operators. Mitigation: co‑investment with local operators/SWFs; local gateways; data residency; security certifications aligned with national frameworks ([SAMENA/DevelopingTelecoms, 2026](https://www.samenacouncil.org/samena_daily_news?news=109546); [Greenberg Traurig, 2026](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2026/3/fcc-seeks-comment-on-satellite-market-access-reciprocity)).

- Capacity planning and congestion: Africa examples show sign‑up halts in major cities due to capacity constraints; careful cell planning and phased launches minimize reputational risk ([Ookla Africa, 2025](https://www.ookla.com/articles/starlink-ssa-q1-2025)).

- Launch cadence risk: Single‑provider dependence creates schedule and price risk. Mitigation: Amazon‑style multi‑launcher procurement with options and cadence buffers ([About Amazon, 2026](https://www.aboutamazon.com/what-we-do/devices-services/amazon-leo)).

- Competitive pricing responses: Incumbents can cut prices or offer save‑desk incentives; attach may lag due to switching friction as seen in U.S. fiber vs. cable. Mitigation: terminal affordability (rentals/installments), partner bundles, and discrete performance advantages (SLAs, D2D) ([SatelliteToday Analysis, 2026](https://interactive.satellitetoday.com/via/march-2026/examining-the-size-of-the-us-residential-broadband-opportunity-for-leo-satcom)).

8. Conclusions and go/no‑go recommendation

- Go—but not as a copy of Starlink. Pursue a region‑led, partnership‑heavy play in SEA and the Middle East with explicit sovereign‑neutral positioning, local gateway investments, and tight terminal/network integration. Use multi‑vendor launch procurement and multi‑orbit partnerships to accelerate enterprise/government routes to revenue. Stage the constellation to match access and anchor‑demand milestones to protect IRR and breakeven timelines ([Deloitte, 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/technology/technology-media-and-telecom-predictions/2026/next-gen-satellite-internet.html); [About Amazon, 2026](https://www.aboutamazon.com/what-we-do/devices-services/amazon-leo); [SAMENA/DevelopingTelecoms, 2026](https://www.samenacouncil.org/samena_daily_news?news=109546)).
