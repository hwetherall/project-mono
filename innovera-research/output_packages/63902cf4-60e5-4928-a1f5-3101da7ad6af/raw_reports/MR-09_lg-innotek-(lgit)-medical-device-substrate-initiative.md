Regulatory and Platform-Policy Landscape for Flexible Substrate & Electrode Components in Medical Devices (CGMS, Neurostimulation, BCI)

Executive Summary

This report analyzes the regulatory and platform-policy environment for LG Innotek (LGIT) to enter high-growth medical device subsegments (Continuous Glucose Monitoring Systems [CGMS], neurostimulation, brain-computer interfaces [BCI]) as a B2B component supplier and/or CDMO. The analysis concludes:

- The U.S. FDA’s transition to the Quality Management System Regulation (QMSR), effective February 2, 2026, formally aligns U.S. device quality requirements with ISO 13485:2016 via incorporation by reference and updates FDA inspections accordingly. This materially reduces trans-Atlantic QMS friction but raises supplier transparency and risk-management expectations; supplier audit reports are now clearly in scope for FDA inspections, increasing the bar for component vendors seeking to be “critical suppliers.” Component vendors with ISO 13485-hardened processes, robust purchasing controls, and audit-ready documentation will see improved access to OEM programs and faster qualification cycles ([FDA QMSR](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/postmarket-requirements-devices/quality-management-system-regulation-qmsr); [FDLI QMSR transition](https://www.fdli.org/2025/12/the-qmsr-transition-new-fda-guidance-and-their-impacts-on-active-implantable-medical-devices/); [AABB QMSR summary](https://www.aabb.org/news-resources/news/article/2026/02/04/fda-implements-quality-management-system-regulation-for-medical-devices)).

- For CGMS and wearable biosensors, updated 2026 FDA General Wellness guidance expands the scope of non-device wearables if claims remain wellness-only and risk is low—creating a preclinical proving ground for materials/processes, but not a replacement for medical-grade pathways. For medical CGMS, the path still requires conformity to ISO 10993 risk-based biocompatibility and full quality system controls. Data and cybersecurity obligations escalate markedly for connected “cyber devices” under FDA’s reissued 2026 cybersecurity guidance implementing FD&C Act §524B (e.g., SBOMs, threat modeling, lifecycle vulnerability management), which may affect LGIT if offering connected modules rather than passive substrates ([Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2026/01/fda-cuts-red-tape-on-clinical-decision-support-software); [Nixon Peabody](https://www.nixonpeabody.com/insights/alerts/2026/01/27/for-2026-fda-signals-shifts-in-digital-health-framework); [FDA Cybersecurity 2026](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/cybersecurity-medical-devices-quality-management-system-considerations-and-content-premarket)).

- In Europe, MDR/IVDR remain the core framework; EUDAMED’s “gradual roll-out” triggers mandatory use by May 28, 2026 (four modules validated), tightening EU market access controls and lifecycle transparency (device/operator registrations, NB certificates). MDR harmonized standards for electro-medical safety (e.g., EN 60601 series) and biocompatibility (EN ISO 10993 series) are central to conformity. Notified Body (NB) capacity, stricter clinical and PMS expectations, and fee structures increase access friction and cost for novel or high-risk implants (e.g., BCI, deep brain stimulation leads), yet favor suppliers who can “medicalize” documentation and help OEMs de-risk NB reviews ([European Commission/MDCG guidance catalog](https://health.ec.europa.eu/medical-devices-sector/new-regulations/guidance-mdcg-endorsed-documents-and-other-guidance_en); [Beaufort CRO on EUDAMED modules](https://beaufortcro.com/article/eudamed-full-functionality-of-first-four-modules-and-what-comes-next-for-ivd-and-medical-device-stakeholders/); [Celegence on EUDAMED mandatory date](https://www.celegence.com/eudamed-mandatory-28-may-2026-medical-device-ivd-manufacturers/)).

- Compliance costs are significant but predictable when scoped early. Benchmarks for ISO 13485 certification, NB assessments, biocompatibility testing, and FDA submissions demonstrate a consistent risk- and complexity-based gradient from wearables/CGMS through implantable neuro devices. Suppliers that deliver traceable, ISO 10993-backed material stacks and process residual controls lower OEM total validation burden and gain pricing power at the component/module tier ([MedEnvoy EU cost ranges](https://medenvoyglobal.com/blog/what-are-the-fees-for-selling-medical-devices-in-europe/); [Qualitiso NB fee comparators](https://www.qualitiso.com/en/comparison-of-notified-body-fees/); [AJProTech compliance budgets](https://ajprotech.com/blog/guides-and-templates/medical-devices-compliance-guide-cost-analysis-budget-strategies-and-fda-and-iso-requirements.html); [Cruxi 10993 testing costs](https://cruxi.ai/pages/directories/biocompatibility-testing/biocompatibility-testing-cost-calculator.html)).

- Broader policy exposure is rising: U.S. tariffs introduced in 2025 increase landed costs on foreign components; the BIOSECURE Act constrains U.S. federal procurement from designated biotechnology “companies of concern,” raising vendor-due-diligence stakes; accelerating PFAS restrictions (with likely sectoral exemptions for essential uses in medical devices) push material substitution roadmaps and tox-chemistry documentation upstream—an opportunity for LGIT to differentiate with PFAS-free or “essential use” defensible material stacks and auditable supply chains ([Medical Design Briefs tariffs analysis](https://www.medicaldesignbriefs.com/component/content/article/52950-u-s-tariffs-implications-and-strategies-for-medtech-manufacturers); [Hogan Lovells on BIOSECURE](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/jpm-2026-ai-compliance-and-biosecure-8089719/); [MoFo BIOSECURE update](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/251218-biosecure-act-update); [Source Intelligence PFAS 2026](https://blog.sourceintelligence.com/pfas-regulations-how-to-remain-compliant); [Certivo PFAS 2025–2026 timeline](https://www.certivo.com/blog-details/global-pfas-regulations-the-2025-2026-compliance-master-guide-for-manufacturers)).

Opinionated recommendation based on the above: LGIT should enter as a medically qualified component/CDMO partner—first targeting CGMS and skin-contact wearables (faster path to revenue with ISO 10993 and process validation), then moving up to implantable neuro electrode arrays with OEM co-development. Rather than pursuing full device OEM status (PMA/CE clinical burdens), LGIT should monetize substrate-level differentiation by “medicalizing” manufacturing (ISO 13485 certification; biocompatible material stacks; validated microfabrication and cleaning; sterilization compatibility; 100% traceability; supplier audit readiness) and offering integrated electrode–substrate subassemblies and testable modules that slot into OEM DHF/tech files. The goal is to become an auditable “critical supplier” whose documentation directly supports OEM PMA/510(k)/MDR submissions and withstands FDA QMSR inspections and NB scrutiny. This strategy aligns with current regulatory shifts favoring ISO 13485 alignment, risk-based purchasing controls, and lifecycle transparency.

Scope and Method

This report synthesizes primary regulatory publications (FDA, European Commission/MDCG) and secondary analyses (FDLI, RAPS, AABB, law firm advisories) current to March 12, 2026, and integrates cost benchmarks from industry sources. It emphasizes near-term shifts (2025–2026) relevant to component suppliers in CGMS, neurostimulation, and BCI segments.

A. Regulatory Landscape

A1. Named regulatory regimes affecting the market

United States (FDA/CDRH)

- Quality Management System Regulation (QMSR), 21 CFR Part 820 (effective Feb 2, 2026), incorporating ISO 13485:2016 by reference and ISO 9000:2015 clause 3; applies to design, production, and lifecycle controls for medical devices, with updated inspection program replacing QSIT ([FDA QMSR](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/postmarket-requirements-devices/quality-management-system-regulation-qmsr); [AABB QMSR](https://www.aabb.org/news-resources/news/article/2026/02/04/fda-implements-quality-management-system-regulation-for-medical-devices)).

- Investigational Device Exemption (IDE) and design controls: Investigational devices are not exempt from design and development requirements under QMSR and ISO 13485 clause 7 (important for OEMs trialing BCI/neurostimulation implants) ([FDA QMSR](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/postmarket-requirements-devices/quality-management-system-regulation-qmsr)).

- Premarket pathways and classification: Most neurological devices are Class II or III; deep brain stimulators and BCI implants are Class III (PMA). Pathway depends on classification and risk controls ([FDA Neurological Devices Overview](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/neurological-devices/regulatory-overview-neurological-devices)).

- Biocompatibility: FDA guidance on ISO 10993-1 requires a risk-based evaluation of the finished device including materials, processing, sterilization, and residuals—directly relevant to flexible substrates and electrode metallization/adhesive processes ([FDA ISO 10993-1 guidance](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/use-international-standard-iso-10993-1-biological-evaluation-medical-devices-part-1-evaluation-and); [FDA Biocompatibility basics](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/biocompatibility-assessment-resource-center/basics-biocompatibility-information-needed-assessment-fda)).

- Recognized consensus standards: FDA recognizes ISO 10993-17:2023 (partial) for toxicological risk assessment, among others—important for extractables/leachables from flexible substrates and thin films ([FDA Recognized Standards](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfStandards/results.cfm?start_search=1&sortcolumn=pdd&productcode=&category=&title=&supportingdocsyn=off&ascapilotyn=off&organization=&referencenumber=&regulationnumber=&recognitionnumber=&effectivedatefrom=&effectivedateto=&pagenum=500)).

- Cybersecurity: Final guidance reissued Feb 2026 aligns with QMSR and addresses FD&C Act §524B “cyber devices,” requiring SBOMs, security risk management, threat modeling, architecture, and lifecycle vulnerability management in premarket submissions (if LGIT supplies connected modules, firmware, or update-capable subsystems) ([FDA Cybersecurity 2026 guidance](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/cybersecurity-medical-devices-quality-management-system-considerations-and-content-premarket); [RAPS coverage](https://www.raps.org/news-and-articles/news-articles/2026/2/fda-reissues-cybersecurity-guidance-to-align-with)).

- Digital health: 2026 updates clarify General Wellness and CDS boundaries; general wellness products using non-invasive sensing without medical claims may avoid device regulation—relevant for preclinical platform demonstrations but not CGMS medical claims ([Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2026/01/fda-cuts-red-tape-on-clinical-decision-support-software); [Faegre Drinker](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2026/1/key-updates-in-fdas-2026-general-wellness-and-clinical-decision-support-software-guidance)).

European Union

- Medical Devices Regulation (EU) 2017/745 (MDR) and In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation (EU) 2017/746 (IVDR): Core framework for CE marking of medical devices and IVDs in the EU, replacing MDD/AIMDD/IVDD. Ongoing updates via MDCG guidance and harmonized standards lists ([EC factsheet](https://health.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2023-07/thirdcountries_factsheet_en_0.pdf); [MDCG guidance catalog](https://health.ec.europa.eu/medical-devices-sector/new-regulations/guidance-mdcg-endorsed-documents-and-other-guidance_en)).

- EUDAMED: Four modules validated; mandatory use as of May 28, 2026 under gradual roll-out (operator/device registration, certificates, vigilance, market surveillance—CI/PS module still in development) ([Celegence EUDAMED decision](https://www.celegence.com/eudamed-mandatory-28-may-2026-medical-device-ivd-manufacturers/); [Beaufort CRO EUDAMED modules](https://beaufortcro.com/article/eudamed-full-functionality-of-first-four-modules-and-what-comes-next-for-ivd-and-medical-device-stakeholders/)).

- Harmonized standards: EN ISO 10993 series (biocompatibility), EN 60601 series (electrical safety/usability/alarms), EN 60601-2-10 (nerve/muscle stimulators), among others that may be cited in conformity assessments for neurostimulation systems and wearable electronics ([MDR harmonized standards list](https://www.medical-device-regulation.eu/mdr-resource-harmonized-standards-lis/); [Casus Consulting 2026 harmonized standards snapshot](https://casusconsulting.com/list-mdr-ivdr-harmonized-standards-common-specifications/)).

- Cybersecurity guidance (MDCG 2019-16) remains a reference in the EU for safety/security-by-design expectations (though not law), often invoked by NBs in technical file reviews ([MDCG catalog](https://health.ec.europa.eu/medical-devices-sector/new-regulations/guidance-mdcg-endorsed-documents-and-other-guidance_en)).

North America (beyond U.S.)

- Mexico: NOM standards and equivalence to “Reference Regulatory Authorities” (e.g., FDA/EU) affect cross-border manufacturing and USMCA rules of origin—relevant if LGIT pursues nearshoring for medical production destined for the U.S. market ([IVEMSA Mexico manufacturing overview](https://www.ivemsa.com/medical-device-manufacturing-mexico-overview/)).

A2. Key regulatory agencies and oversight bodies

- United States: FDA (CDRH, with some CBER cross-over for combination products); applies QMSR inspections (Program 7382.850) and enforces FD&C Act §524B for cybersecurity in connected devices ([FDA QMSR inspections note](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/postmarket-requirements-devices/quality-management-system-regulation-qmsr); [FDA Cybersecurity guidance 2026](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/cybersecurity-medical-devices-quality-management-system-considerations-and-content-premarket)).

- European Union: European Commission (legislative and EUDAMED stewardship), MDCG (guidance), Notified Bodies (conformity assessment under MDR), National Competent Authorities (market surveillance and vigilance). EUDAMED operationalizes operator and device registrations, NB certificates, vigilance, and postmarket data ([MDCG guidance catalog](https://health.ec.europa.eu/medical-devices-sector/new-regulations/guidance-mdcg-endorsed-documents-and-other-guidance_en); [Beaufort CRO EUDAMED modules](https://beaufortcro.com/article/eudamed-full-functionality-of-first-four-modules-and-what-comes-next-for-ivd-and-medical-device-stakeholders/)).

- Standards bodies: ISO (e.g., ISO 13485; ISO 10993 series), IEC (e.g., IEC 60601 series), AAMI and ANSI/AAMI U.S. adoptions; IMDRF (essential principles and alignment) ([FDA QMSR ISO incorporation](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/postmarket-requirements-devices/quality-management-system-regulation-qmsr); [IMDRF essential principles mention via FDA QMSR resources](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/postmarket-requirements-devices/quality-management-system-regulation-qmsr)).

A3. Regulatory shifts with enacting body and effective date

Key recent changes affecting component suppliers and CDMOs:

- FDA QMSR Final Rule (enacted by FDA, effective February 2, 2026, U.S.): Incorporates ISO 13485:2016 by reference; IDE devices not exempt from design/development requirements; inspection program replaced QSIT with updated Compliance Program 7382.850 on Feb 2, 2026 ([FDA QMSR](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/postmarket-requirements-devices/quality-management-system-regulation-qmsr)).

- FDA draft guidance (Quality Management System Information for Certain Premarket Submission Reviews) – October 27, 2025 (U.S.): Signals that ISO 13485-mapped QMS information will be scrutinized in PMA/HDE and possibly other submissions; underscores purchasing controls and risk-based supplier qualification. Supplier audit reports are inspectable, implying OEMs may be asked to reconcile purchasing controls in submissions with actual supplier-audit documentation ([Alston & Bird summary](https://www.alston.com/en/insights/publications/2025/11/fda-shift-qmsr-transition-medical-devices); [FDLI analysis](https://www.fdli.org/2025/12/the-qmsr-transition-new-fda-guidance-and-their-impacts-on-active-implantable-medical-devices/)).

- FDA Cybersecurity Guidance reissued (enacted by FDA, issued February 3, 2026, U.S.): Aligns with QMSR; implements §524B expectations for “cyber devices”—including SBOMs, exploitability-oriented security risk management, architecture disclosures, threat models, and documented patching/vulnerability processes in premarket submissions ([FDA Cybersecurity guidance 2026](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/cybersecurity-medical-devices-quality-management-system-considerations-and-content-premarket); [RAPS](https://www.raps.org/news-and-articles/news-articles/2026/2/fda-reissues-cybersecurity-guidance-to-align-with)).

- FDA Recognized Consensus Standards (ISO 10993-17:2023 partial recognition – Dec 18, 2023): Elevates the latest toxicological risk assessment expectations for device constituents—critical for flexible substrates where extractables/leachables and process residuals must be characterized ([FDA Recognized Standards](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfStandards/results.cfm?start_search=1&sortcolumn=pdd&productcode=&category=&title=&supportingdocsyn=off&ascapilotyn=off&organization=&referencenumber=&regulationnumber=&recognitionnumber=&effectivedatefrom=&effectivedateto=&pagenum=500)).

- EUDAMED mandatory use (enacted by European Commission under gradual roll-out framework, mandatory May 28, 2026, EU): Four modules validated; manufacturers and NBs must use EUDAMED for registrations and certificate lifecycle management, centralizing market access and surveillance workflows ([Celegence](https://www.celegence.com/eudamed-mandatory-28-may-2026-medical-device-ivd-manufacturers/); [Beaufort CRO](https://beaufortcro.com/article/eudamed-full-functionality-of-first-four-modules-and-what-comes-next-for-ivd-and-medical-device-stakeholders/)).

- 2026 FDA Digital Health updates (General Wellness and CDS guidance updates – Jan 2026, U.S.): Expands non-invasive, low-risk general wellness products (including sensor-based wearables) that fall outside device regulation if avoiding medical claims; clarifies CDS non-device boundaries when clinicians can independently review recommendations using validated data ([Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2026/01/fda-cuts-red-tape-on-clinical-decision-support-software); [Nixon Peabody](https://www.nixonpeabody.com/insights/alerts/2026/01/27/for-2026-fda-signals-shifts-in-digital-health-framework); [Faegre Drinker](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2026/1/key-updates-in-fdas-2026-general-wellness-and-clinical-decision-support-software-guidance)).

A4. Demand-side effects of policy shifts

- ISO 13485–aligned QMSR (U.S.) lowers friction for ISO 13485–certified component vendors selling into U.S. OEMs, creating incremental demand for suppliers that can deliver audit-ready, risk-managed purchasing controls and documented traceability compatible with FDA inspections and PMA submissions ([FDA QMSR](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/postmarket-requirements-devices/quality-management-system-regulation-qmsr); [FDLI QMSR transition](https://www.fdli.org/2025/12/the-qmsr-transition-new-fda-guidance-and-their-impacts-on-active-implantable-medical-devices/)).

- 2026 General Wellness guidance expands non-device sensor wearables. This can stimulate upstream demand for flexible electrodes and substrates in consumer wellness pilots; however, transitioning from wellness to medical claims (e.g., CGMS) still requires full device regulatory pathways (biocompatibility, validation, clinical evidence) ([Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2026/01/fda-cuts-red-tape-on-clinical-decision-support-software); [Faegre Drinker](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2026/1/key-updates-in-fdas-2026-general-wellness-and-clinical-decision-support-software-guidance)).

- Cybersecurity obligations under §524B increase OEM preference for suppliers of subsystems with security-by-design practices and complete SBOMs. Component suppliers that provide firmware/software or connectivity will see increased demand if they provide auditable cybersecurity artifacts that reduce OEM submission burden ([FDA Cybersecurity 2026](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/cybersecurity-medical-devices-quality-management-system-considerations-and-content-premarket)).

- EUDAMED mandatory use consolidates EU lifecycle transparency and may accelerate the shift from legacy MDD/AIMDD devices to MDR-certified portfolios, driving OEM refresh projects and supplier requalification—demanding robust documentation from component vendors ([Celegence](https://www.celegence.com/eudamed-mandatory-28-may-2026-medical-device-ivd-manufacturers/)).

A5. Access-side effects of policy shifts

- Supplier audit visibility: Under QMSR and draft QMS premarket guidance, purchasing controls disclosed in PMA may be reconciled with supplier audit reports during FDA inspections—raising the qualification bar for component/CDMO suppliers and closing access to vendors without ISO 13485-grade systems and risk-managed supply chains ([FDLI analysis](https://www.fdli.org/2025/12/the-qmsr-transition-new-fda-guidance-and-their-impacts-on-active-implantable-medical-devices/)).

- PMA change control friction: For Class III neuro devices, manufacturing site moves, critical component changes, or process changes often trigger PMA supplements or 30-day notices; this elongates requalification timelines and motivates OEMs to prefer suppliers with stable, well-validated processes and documented sterilization/material equivalence—direct access barrier for new entrants without medicalized processes ([Hogan Lovells JDSupra](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/jpm-2026-ai-compliance-and-biosecure-8089719/); [Matric supply chain note](https://www.matric.com/medical-device-supply-chain)).

- EUDAMED ubiquity and NB constraints: EU market access increasingly depends on NB capacity and well-prepared technical documentation. Component vendors that supply standardized “regulatory data packs” (materials, processing residuals, sterilization compatibility, 10993 chemical characterizations) become more reachable/preferred by OEMs and NBs, while those who cannot support MDR PMS and vigilance integration become less reachable ([MDCG guidance catalog](https://health.ec.europa.eu/medical-devices-sector/new-regulations/guidance-mdcg-endorsed-documents-and-other-guidance_en); [Celegence EUDAMED](https://www.celegence.com/eudamed-mandatory-28-may-2026-medical-device-ivd-manufacturers/)).

A6. Compliance cost benchmarks

The following represent indicative ranges for planning; actual costs vary by device class, novelty, and maturity of QMS. Sources provide ranges rather than fixed fees.

- ISO 13485 QMS development and certification: $10,000–$120,000 initial (organization size, gap to certification) ([MedEnvoy fees](https://medenvoyglobal.com/blog/what-are-the-fees-for-selling-medical-devices-in-europe/); [AJProTech budgets](https://ajprotech.com/blog/guides-and-templates/medical-devices-compliance-guide-cost-analysis-budget-strategies-and-fda-and-iso-requirements.html)).

- EU MDR CE marking and NB costs:
  - Class IIa: ~$15,000–$40,000
  - Class IIb: ~$40,000–$80,000
  - Class III: often >$100,000 (complex clinical evaluation, extensive NB scrutiny)
  - Ongoing NB surveillance audits: ~$5,000–$20,000 annually
  - EU Authorized Representative: ~$2,000–$10,000 annually ([MedEnvoy fees](https://medenvoyglobal.com/blog/what-are-the-fees-for-selling-medical-devices-in-europe/); [Eurodev MDR cost context](https://www.eurodev.com/blog/cost-of-obtaining-the-european-medical-device-regulations); [MedEnvoy ongoing fees](https://medenvoyglobal.com/blog/what-are-the-fees-for-selling-medical-devices-in-europe/); [Qualitiso NB fee comparisons](https://www.qualitiso.com/en/comparison-of-notified-body-fees/)).

- FDA submissions:
  - 510(k): $50,000–$500,000 (excluding internal labor); typical 6–12 months timeline
  - PMA: $2–$5 million (including clinical studies), multiyear timelines ([AJProTech FDA costs](https://ajprotech.com/blog/guides-and-templates/medical-devices-compliance-guide-cost-analysis-budget-strategies-and-fda-and-iso-requirements.html)).

- Biocompatibility testing (ISO 10993):
  - Cytotoxicity (10993-5): ~$800–$1,800
  - Broader programs (including 10993-1 risk assessment and 10993-17 tox risk) scale significantly with contact type/duration and material stacks; complex implantables require extractables/leachables and toxicological risk assessment per FDA-recognized 10993-17:2023 ([Cruxi biocompatibility costs](https://cruxi.ai/pages/directories/biocompatibility-testing/biocompatibility-testing-cost-calculator.html); [FDA Recognized Standards](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfStandards/results.cfm?start_search=1&sortcolumn=pdd&productcode=&category=&title=&supportingdocsyn=off&ascapilotyn=off&organization=&referencenumber=&regulationnumber=&recognitionnumber=&effectivedatefrom=&effectivedateto=&pagenum=500)).

- Verification & validation for a typical Class II device (non-implant): $150,000–$350,000; early audits $15,000–$30,000 (skipping often triples rework) ([AJProTech V&V](https://ajprotech.com/blog/guides-and-templates/medical-devices-compliance-guide-cost-analysis-budget-strategies-and-fda-and-iso-requirements.html)).

- Ongoing yearly compliance: $25,000–$100,000 (PMS, vigilances, audits, complaint handling) ([AJProTech](https://ajprotech.com/blog/guides-and-templates/medical-devices-compliance-guide-cost-analysis-budget-strategies-and-fda-and-iso-requirements.html); [MedEnvoy ongoing fees](https://medenvoyglobal.com/blog/what-are-the-fees-for-selling-medical-devices-in-europe/)).

Cost takeaways for LGIT:

- A medicalized component business requires ISO 13485 certification and biocompatibility readiness (risk-based 10993-1, -17, -18 chemical characterization) even if LGIT is not the finished-device applicant. Offering OEM-ready testing reports and tox assessments reduces OEM total cost and NB/FDA friction—monetizable as premium pricing and faster design-ins ([FDA 10993-1](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/use-international-standard-iso-10993-1-biological-evaluation-medical-devices-part-1-evaluation-and); [FDA Recognized 10993-17:2023](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfStandards/results.cfm?start_search=1&sortcolumn=pdd&productcode=&category=&title=&supportingdocsyn=off&ascapilotyn=off&organization=&referencenumber=&regulationnumber=&recognitionnumber=&effectivedatefrom=&effectivedateto=&pagenum=500)).

A7. Data privacy and cybersecurity requirements shaping product design

- FDA Cybersecurity (2026): For any device or module qualifying as a “cyber device” (broadly, software that can connect to the internet), premarket submissions must include SBOMs, threat models, penetration testing, architecture views, vulnerability management plans, and a separate security risk management process distinct from ISO 14971 safety risk (exploitability-focused) ([FDA Cybersecurity 2026](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/cybersecurity-medical-devices-quality-management-system-considerations-and-content-premarket); [RAPS](https://www.raps.org/news-and-articles/news-articles/2026/2/fda-reissues-cybersecurity-guidance-to-align-with)).

- MDR cybersecurity: While not codified as a single binding rule, MDCG 2019-16 outlines cybersecurity expectations under MDR/IVDR; NBs increasingly expect security risk management documentation for connected devices (less relevant to passive components but crucial if LGIT supplies connected subassemblies) ([MDCG catalog](https://health.ec.europa.eu/medical-devices-sector/new-regulations/guidance-mdcg-endorsed-documents-and-other-guidance_en)).

- Data privacy: While not directly covered by the provided sources, note that OEMs will generally expect component/module suppliers that process data (e.g., embedded controllers with logs) to support privacy-by-design and provide technical files enabling OEM compliance; absence of direct citations limits elaboration here.

B. Platform Dynamics

B8. Named platform policies or rule changes affecting the market

- EUDAMED as regulatory platform: Four validated modules (economic operators/actors, UDI/device registration, Notified Bodies & Certificates, Vigilance/Market Surveillance) now anchor EU market access, creating system-level dependencies for registrations, certificate lifecycle events, and PMS reporting. The Clinical Investigations module remains under development. Mandatory use as of May 28, 2026, functionally “platformizes” EU device lifecycle management ([Beaufort CRO](https://beaufortcro.com/article/eudamed-full-functionality-of-first-four-modules-and-what-comes-next-for-ivd-and-medical-device-stakeholders/); [Celegence](https://www.celegence.com/eudamed-mandatory-28-may-2026-medical-device-ivd-manufacturers/)).

- FDA “recognized standards” database acts as a de facto premarket platform: Submissions referencing current recognized versions (e.g., ISO 10993-17:2023 partial) may see smoother reviews; outdated references risk additional queries, shaping test strategy and lab/vendor selection ([FDA Recognized Standards](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfStandards/results.cfm?start_search=1&sortcolumn=pdd&productcode=&category=&title=&supportingdocsyn=off&ascapilotyn=off&organization=&referencenumber=&regulationnumber=&recognitionnumber=&effectivedatefrom=&effectivedateto=&pagenum=500)).

- FDA submission acceptance checklists (RTA) and cybersecurity guidance define file-completeness “platform requirements” particularly for software-containing submissions—impacting module-level suppliers contributing to OEM submissions ([Censinet references to FDA checklists](https://censinet.com/perspectives/fda-cybersecurity-guidance-key-updates-2025)).

- App distribution ecosystems (illustrative): Even non-device wellness apps interfacing with CGMs must meet app store privacy disclosures and data-handling transparency; while not regulatory, platform policies steer product claims and integrations. The cited App Store page for a glucose tracker illustrates third-party CGM integrations—a reminder that platform gatekeepers (Apple/Google) can shape market access for companion software and data flows related to sensors ([Apple App Store page example](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/glucose-blood-sugar-tracker/id1135157609)).

B9. Platform changes with owner and effective date

- EUDAMED mandatory activation (European Commission; May 28, 2026): Requires actors to use validated modules for EU market operations; impacts OEMs, NBs, and economic operators (including CDMOs registered as manufacturers/importers where applicable) ([Celegence](https://www.celegence.com/eudamed-mandatory-28-may-2026-medical-device-ivd-manufacturers/)).

- FDA cybersecurity guidance reissue (FDA; Feb 3, 2026): Aligns documentation requirements with QMSR and §524B, operationalizing SBOMs and lifecycle cybersecurity obligations as premarket prerequisites for “cyber devices” ([FDA Cybersecurity 2026](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/cybersecurity-medical-devices-quality-management-system-considerations-and-content-premarket)).

- General Wellness guidance update (FDA; Jan 2026): Expands non-device low-risk wearables (owner: FDA), indirectly affecting software platform policies by discouraging medical claims in app-store listings for wellness devices ([Nixon Peabody](https://www.nixonpeabody.com/insights/alerts/2026/01/27/for-2026-fda-signals-shifts-in-digital-health-framework)).

C. Broader Policy Exposure

C10. Environmental, trade, and labor rule exposure

- PFAS restrictions (EU and U.S. states): 2025–2026 acceleration in PFAS regulatory actions; EU is moving toward a sector-phased restriction with likely “essential use” exemptions for medical devices. U.S. states (e.g., Maine, Minnesota, Colorado) ramp reporting and bans with “currently unavoidable use” exemptions under review in 2026. Implication: Flexible substrates and electrode stacks relying on PFAS chemistries face substitution pressure and documentary burdens. Suppliers that deliver PFAS-free alternatives or robust essential-use justifications gain competitive advantage and secure continuity (especially in EU MDR conformity where material risk management is scrutinized) ([Source Intelligence PFAS 2026](https://blog.sourceintelligence.com/pfas-regulations-how-to-remain-compliant); [Certivo PFAS timeline](https://www.certivo.com/blog-details/global-pfas-regulations-the-2025-2026-compliance-master-guide-for-manufacturers)).

- U.S. tariffs 2025: Sweeping tariff hikes (e.g., 125% on Chinese goods; 10% baseline on many partners) raise input costs and motivate onshoring/nearshoring strategies. Medtech suppliers face higher BOM costs and potential retaliation risks in export markets; reshoring incentives could benefit U.S./Mexico investments (USMCA rules of origin apply) ([Medical Design Briefs](https://www.medicaldesignbriefs.com/component/content/article/52950-u-s-tariffs-implications-and-strategies-for-medtech-manufacturers); [IVEMSA Mexico NOMs & USMCA](https://www.ivemsa.com/medical-device-manufacturing-mexico-overview/)).

- BIOSECURE Act (enacted Dec 18, 2025 via FY2026 NDAA): Restricts U.S. executive agency procurement from “biotechnology companies of concern.” Device OEMs with federal contracts may pressure supply chains to avoid listed entities; due diligence and supply chain mapping become contract-critical for CDMOs/components. Impacts strategic sourcing (e.g., fab equipment/services) and co-development partnerships ([Hogan Lovells](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/jpm-2026-ai-compliance-and-biosecure-8089719/); [MoFo](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/251218-biosecure-act-update)).

C11. Subsidies, grants, and public incentives

- European funding programs (EU4Health, Digital Europe Programme, Horizon Europe, etc.) inject funds into digital health infrastructure and innovation, supporting markets for interoperable devices and data platforms—indirectly boosting demand for advanced sensor components integrated into funded systems ([European Commission – EU funding for digital health](https://health.ec.europa.eu/ehealth-digital-health-and-care/eu-funding-digital-health_en)).

- Canada Global Innovation Clusters: Co-investment programs backing digital/advanced manufacturing and AI projects; while not device-specific, they can subsidize technology transfer and pilot lines relevant to advanced flexible electronics in medtech applications ([ISED Canada – Global Innovation Clusters](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/global-innovation-clusters/en/global-innovation-clusters)).

- U.S. innovation support (contextual): FDA’s posture (e.g., Emerging Technology) and BARDA funding (as referenced in market analyses) historically support advanced manufacturing and pandemic response, indirectly strengthening domestic CDMO ecosystems (strategic context, not a direct citation of a current grant) ([MarketDataForecast CDMO context](https://www.marketdataforecast.com/market-reports/cdmo-market)).

Strategic Implications and Recommendations for LG Innotek

1) Choose the entry tier: Component/CDMO over full OEM

- Rationale: PMA-class neuro and BCI systems impose long, capital-intensive clinical and regulatory cycles. A component/CDMO approach leverages LGIT’s strengths (10 μm photolithography, flexible FPCB, R2R production) while avoiding PMA ownership. QMSR-ISO 13485 harmonization increases OEM appetite for ISO 13485–certified “critical suppliers” that can withstand FDA inspections and NB audits. LGIT can monetize by shortening OEM time-to-approval via documentation, not by taking on full clinical risk ([FDA QMSR](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/postmarket-requirements-devices/quality-management-system-regulation-qmsr); [FDLI on supplier audits](https://www.fdli.org/2025/12/the-qmsr-transition-new-fda-guidance-and-their-impacts-on-active-implantable-medical-devices/)).

2) Stage the market sequence: CGMS/wearables first, implants next

- Phase 1 (CGMS/wearables): Prioritize skin-contact electrode substrates and sensor interposers. Achieve ISO 13485 certification; build ISO 10993–justified material stacks (e.g., Au, Pt, Ag/AgCl, parylene C) with chemical characterization (10993-18) and tox risk (10993-17:2023). Validate manufacturing cleaning (new solvent compatibility) and residuals (align with FDA expectations for finished-form risk assessments). Demonstrate sterilization compatibility (ethylene oxide, gamma, E-beam) where required ([FDA Biocompatibility basics](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/biocompatibility-assessment-resource-center/basics-biocompatibility-information-needed-assessment-fda); [FDA Recognized 10993-17](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfStandards/results.cfm?start_search=1&sortcolumn=pdd&productcode=&category=&title=&supportingdocsyn=off&ascapilotyn=off&organization=&referencenumber=&regulationnumber=&recognitionnumber=&effectivedatefrom=&effectivedateto=&pagenum=500)).

- Phase 2 (Neuro/BCI implants): Build implant-ready electrode arrays (higher channel density, conformal flexible substrates), with attention to IEC 60601 implications at system level for OEMs (2-10 for stimulators; collateral 60601-1-6 usability; -1-8 alarms as applicable) and neurosurgical implant materials compatibility. Offer co-development with OEMs for hermetic feedthrough interfaces and long-term encapsulation strategies; ensure process change controls suitable for PMA supplements or 30-day notices to avoid requalification shocks ([MDR harmonized standards page – EN 60601-2-10](https://www.medical-device-regulation.eu/mdr-resource-harmonized-standards-lis/); [Hogan Lovells JDSupra change control note](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/jpm-2026-ai-compliance-and-biosecure-8089719/)).

3) Build a “regulatory data pack” as product

- For each substrate/electrode stack, bundle:
  - ISO 10993-1 biological evaluation plan and results (cytotoxicity, sensitization/irritation, systemic endpoints as indicated by contact type/duration), including 10993-18 chemical characterization and 10993-17 tox risk assessment aligning with FDA-recognized editions.
  - Processing residual analysis and acceptance criteria; solvent/cleaner change validations with comparability data.
  - Sterilization compatibility evidence and residuals (e.g., EtO ETO/EO residuals in line with applicable norms; though not cited, align with OEM standards).
  - Lot-level traceability, DHR exemplars, and change-control matrices supporting OEM PMA/510(k)/MDR technical files.
  - Supplier audit readiness package (quality manual cross-walk to ISO 13485 and QMSR; purchasing controls; CAPA and risk management) anticipating FDA inspection alignment post-QMSR ([FDA Biocompatibility basics](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/biocompatibility-assessment-resource-center/basics-biocompatibility-information-needed-assessment-fda); [FDLI supplier audit emphasis](https://www.fdli.org/2025/12/the-qmsr-transition-new-fda-guidance-and-their-impacts-on-active-implantable-medical-devices/); [FDA QMSR](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/postmarket-requirements-devices/quality-management-system-regulation-qmsr)).

4) Decide on connected modules carefully

- If adding electronics/firmware (e.g., smart electrode modules, reader interposers), LGIT must conform to FDA cyber device expectations (SBOMs, threat modeling, continuous vulnerability management) and contribute cybersecurity documentation to OEM submissions—significantly increasing QMS scope. If strategic, pursue; otherwise, maintain “dumb” subassemblies to avoid §524B exposure initially ([FDA Cybersecurity 2026](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/cybersecurity-medical-devices-quality-management-system-considerations-and-content-premarket)).

5) “Medicalize” supply chains under PFAS, tariffs, and BIOSECURE constraints

- Establish material substitution roadmaps for PFAS-containing chemistries; where unavoidable, prepare essential-use dossiers for EU and CUU justifications for U.S. state regimes. Build vendor transparency (full material disclosures and PFAS screening) into purchasing controls and demonstrate to OEMs as value-add ([Source Intelligence PFAS](https://blog.sourceintelligence.com/pfas-regulations-how-to-remain-compliant); [Certivo PFAS 2025–2026](https://www.certivo.com/blog-details/global-pfas-regulations-the-2025-2026-compliance-master-guide-for-manufacturers)).

- Evaluate onshoring/nearshoring (e.g., Mexico) to mitigate tariffs and support USMCA origin. Ensure NOMs compliance and alignment with FDA/EU equivalency where applicable for cross-border production ([Medical Design Briefs – tariffs](https://www.medicaldesignbriefs.com/component/content/article/52950-u-s-tariffs-implications-and-strategies-for-medtech-manufacturers); [IVEMSA – NOMs & USMCA](https://www.ivemsa.com/medical-device-manufacturing-mexico-overview/)).

- Map suppliers against BIOSECURE Act considerations if serving federal-channel OEMs; requalify as needed ([MoFo BIOSECURE](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/251218-biosecure-act-update)).

6) Partnerships and route-to-market

- Target OEMs and CDMOs with immediate fit: CGMS leaders (Abbott, Dexcom, Medtronic), neurostim OEMs (Medtronic, Boston Scientific), BCI program integrators (Blackrock Neurotech, Synchron), and Tier-1 CDMOs (Jabil, Flex, Integer) needing specialized fine-pitch, flexible electrode manufacturing. Offer co-development and DFM to bake in biocompatible stacks and sterilization compatibility early, avoiding costly late-stage changes (noted as a major cost trap) ([AJProTech – avoid late rework](https://ajprotech.com/blog/guides-and-templates/medical-devices-compliance-guide-cost-analysis-budget-strategies-and-fda-and-iso-requirements.html)).

- Become a “critical subcontractor” under EU MDR recognized by NBs: codify quality agreements (responsibility for CAPA, change control, complaint support) and be NB-audit ready, as NBs frequently audit critical suppliers under MDR ([Syrma Johari CMO compliance guide](https://syrmajoharimedtech.com/medical-device-contract-manufacturing-compliance-guide/)).

- Provide change-control workflows aligned with PMA supplement categories and 30-day notices to minimize OEM regulatory friction on component updates—stable processes are a commercial differentiator ([Hogan Lovells JDSupra](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/jpm-2026-ai-compliance-and-biosecure-8089719/); [Matric](https://www.matric.com/medical-device-supply-chain)).

Policy-Shift Ledger

- Demand creation
  - FDA QMSR alignment with ISO 13485 (U.S., Feb 2026): Increases OEM demand for ISO 13485–certified, audit-ready component suppliers that reduce compliance risk ([FDA QMSR](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/postmarket-requirements-devices/quality-management-system-regulation-qmsr)).
  - 2026 General Wellness guidance (U.S.): Expands non-device wearables, stimulating upstream sensor/electrode trials and partnerships without immediate device compliance burdens ([Nixon Peabody](https://www.nixonpeabody.com/insights/alerts/2026/01/27/for-2026-fda-signals-shifts-in-digital-health-framework)).
  - EU/EUDAMED consolidation: Drives OEM portfolio refresh and supplier requalification—opportunity for “medicalized” component vendors ([Celegence](https://www.celegence.com/eudamed-mandatory-28-may-2026-medical-device-ivd-manufacturers/)).

- Access friction
  - Supplier audit transparency under QMSR: Raises bar for component suppliers; non-ISO 13485 vendors face exclusion ([FDLI](https://www.fdli.org/2025/12/the-qmsr-transition-new-fda-guidance-and-their-impacts-on-active-implantable-medical-devices/)).
  - FDA Cybersecurity 2026: Significant premarket documentation if supplying connected modules; SBOM and security lifecycle requirements increase burden ([FDA Cybersecurity](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/cybersecurity-medical-devices-quality-management-system-considerations-and-content-premarket)).
  - PMA change controls: Manufacturing/site changes cause supplements/30-day notices; OEMs avoid unstable suppliers ([Hogan Lovells JDSupra](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/jpm-2026-ai-compliance-and-biosecure-8089719/)).
  - EU NB capacity and fees: Heightened scrutiny and cost under MDR prolong time-to-market for new suppliers without turnkey documentation ([MedEnvoy](https://medenvoyglobal.com/blog/what-are-the-fees-for-selling-medical-devices-in-europe/)).

- Neutral/background regulation (baseline constraints)
  - ISO 10993 risk-based biocompatibility: Emphasis on process residuals and extractables/leachables; necessary foundation for all tiers of products touching patients ([FDA 10993-1](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/use-international-standard-iso-10993-1-biological-evaluation-medical-devices-part-1-evaluation-and)).
  - IEC/EN 60601 series: System-level safety/usability collateral standards relevant to OEMs (less direct for component suppliers; still shapes DFM and test). ([MDR harmonized standards page](https://www.medical-device-regulation.eu/mdr-resource-harmonized-standards-lis/)).
