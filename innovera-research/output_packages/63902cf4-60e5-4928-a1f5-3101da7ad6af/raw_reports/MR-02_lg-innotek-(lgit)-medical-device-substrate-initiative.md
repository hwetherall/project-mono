A. Route-to-Market and Channel Structure

A1. Dominant route-to-market types and why they dominate

- Direct OEM enterprise sales (dominant): Finished-device manufacturers (e.g., Dexcom, Abbott, Medtronic, Boston Scientific) must qualify and control “critical suppliers” under written purchasing controls. Under the FDA’s QMSR (effective Feb 2, 2026), FDA explicitly notes that while QMSR provisions do not apply directly to component manufacturers, finished device manufacturers must assess supplier capability, and certification alone “may not provide adequate assurances … without further evaluation.” FDA also states supplier controls can be achieved via audits, review of historical data, monitoring/trending, and product testing—requiring bilateral, often long-term, direct relationships rather than transactional procurement ([FDA, 2024](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/02/02/2024-01709/medical-devices-quality-system-regulation-amendments)).

- CDMO partnerships (co-dominant for many OEMs, especially startups): The medical device CDMO market is expanding rapidly as OEMs outsource complex subassemblies and components. Integrated CDMOs like Jabil, Flex, Phillips-Medisize, Integer, Viant, Sanmina, and Celestica provide mechatronics, microfabrication, assembly, sterilization, and supply-chain services. OEMs increasingly rely on CDMOs for specialist flexible electronics and electrode capabilities, making CDMOs essential channel partners for component suppliers that can slot into their qualified supply chains ([CSG Talent, 2026](https://www.csgtalent.com/insights/blog/medical-devices-cdmo-market-2026/); [LinkedIn CDMO Market Leaders, 2026](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/medical-device-cdmo-market-size-2026-ai-technology-outlook-ydugc/)).

- Inclusion on Approved Supplier Lists (ASL)/Approved Vendor Lists (AVL) (gating mechanism, not a sales route per se): Medical device companies must maintain ASL procedures under FDA QSR/QMSR and ISO 13485, and suppliers are added following risk-based qualification, questionnaires, audits (especially Tier 1/Tier 2), and quality agreements with audit clauses (per MDR/NBOG expectations). This gate means enterprise-direct and CDMO-led routes require formal onboarding before revenue flows; component deals rarely occur through marketplaces because procurement must be auditable and compliant ([Definitive Healthcare, n.d.](https://www.definitivehc.com/resources/glossary/approved-supplier-list); [Greenlight Guru, 2025](https://www.greenlight.guru/blog/approved-supplier-list)).

- Marketplaces/self-serve (marginal for critical components): While consumer electronics often leverage marketplaces, critical medical components—especially those contacting the body or in implantables—are qualified through controlled supply chains with ASL/AVL, change-control, and periodic audits, which are incompatible with anonymous marketplaces. OEMs must be able to demonstrate supplier control to regulators and Notified Bodies; supplier agreements often grant Notified Body audit access to the supplier itself (per NBOG guidance), further reinforcing direct or CDMO-mediated channels ([Greenlight Guru, 2025](https://www.greenlight.guru/blog/approved-supplier-list)).

Evidence: the need for OEM purchasing controls and supplier audits; PMA submissions may reference supplier selection and audits, and FDA can inspect supplier audit reports under QMSR ([FDLI, 2025](https://www.fdli.org/2025/12/the-qmsr-transition-new-fda-guidance-and-their-impacts-on-active-implantable-medical-devices/)). A3. Marketplace or platform access requirements

- ASL/AVL documentation and qualification: Suppliers must be added to the OEM’s ASL/AVL via documented procedures, including supplier questionnaires, risk-tiering, and (for Tier 1 and many Tier 2) onboarding audits; typically, ongoing audits occur every 1–2 years for Tier 1 and up to every 3 years for Tier 2. All qualification decisions and evidence must be documented and auditable (per FDA QMSR/ISO 13485 purchasing controls) ([Greenlight Guru, 2025](https://www.greenlight.guru/blog/approved-supplier-list)).

- Quality agreements with audit clause: Under EU MDR, Notified Bodies expect audit rights to critical suppliers. Greenlight Guru points to NBOG and MDSAP as resources for drafting supplier agreements granting notified-body access—effectively a participation requirement in EU supply chains ([Greenlight Guru, 2025](https://www.greenlight.guru/blog/approved-supplier-list)).

- Compliance documentation: For materials/electrical subassemblies sold as components or modules in EU and other markets, documentation to support RoHS, REACH (and country-specific RoHS equivalents), and basic safety/EMC conformance (for active modules) must be available. UL Solutions and Nemko report updated RoHS and global market access requirements (e.g., EU delegated directives on lead-related exemptions applicable in 2026, China RoHS tightened limits from Jan 2026), underscoring the need for component-level documentation and, where applicable, SDoC registrations for certain markets ([UL Solutions, 2026](https://www.ul.com/news/global-market-access-regulatory-newsletter-q4-2025); [Source Intelligence, 2026](https://blog.sourceintelligence.com/eu-rohs-compliance); [Nemko, 2025](https://www.nemko.com/blog/some-recent-and-upcoming-regulatory-changes-in-china)).

B. Access and Compliance Gates

B4. Jurisdiction-specific licensing or compliance requirements (North America, Europe; Global)

- FDA QMSR (21 CFR Part 820, effective Feb 2, 2026): Harmonizes with ISO 13485:2016 by incorporating it by reference. Manufacturers must have a QMS compliant with ISO 13485 plus FDA-specific applicable requirements (e.g., MDR reporting, UDI, device tracking). While QMSR does not directly apply to component makers, OEMs’ purchasing controls will require supplier evaluation, and under QMSR, FDA can inspect supplier audit reports in PMA contexts—functionally raising the bar for supplier quality documentation and audit readiness ([FDA, 2024](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/02/02/2024-01709/medical-devices-quality-system-regulation-amendments); [FDLI, 2025](https://www.fdli.org/2025/12/the-qmsr-transition-new-fda-guidance-and-their-impacts-on-active-implantable-medical-devices/); [AAMI, 2026](https://aami.org/news/qmsr-understanding-part-820-10-requirements-for-a-quality-management-system-and-the-applicable-regulatory-requirements/)).

- ISO 13485 alignment and expectations: The FDA clarified it will not require ISO 13485 certification and will not issue ISO certificates via inspections. However, many OEMs will expect ISO 13485–certified suppliers for critical components as a “security blanket,” especially post-QMSR alignment. European OEMs and Notified Bodies under MDR/IVDR already expect ISO 13485 across critical suppliers ([Compliancenavigator/BSI, 2026](https://compliancenavigator.bsigroup.com/en/medicaldeviceblog/the-new-fda-21-cfr-part-820--quality-management-system-regulation/); [Degrandson, 2025](https://info.degrandson.com/blog/iso-13485-requirements-medical-device-suppliers)).

- EU MDR (2017/745) supplier ecosystem requirements: Importers and distributors have legal obligations (Articles 13 and 14), including verifying CE marking, EU Declaration of Conformity, UDI assignment, and labeling—making supply-chain compliance auditable beyond the manufacturer. While component suppliers are upstream of these roles, MDR implementation (and MDCG Q&A) drives OEMs and Notified Bodies to push audit rights and traceability into critical supplier agreements ([Medical Device Regulation EU—Articles 13 & 14](https://www.medical-device-regulation.eu/mdr-article-13-general-obligations-of-importers/); [RegDesk, 2024](https://www.regdesk.co/blog/mdcg-guidance-on-articles-13-14-of-mdr-and-ivdr-obligations/)).

- Biocompatibility and safety standards: For patient-contact electrodes, ISO 10993 biological evaluation applies; for active modules, IEC 60601 series (EMC/safety) and, for breathing gas interfaces, ISO 18562, are relevant. MDR uses “harmonized standards” as conformity references; suppliers must support OEMs with test data and material declarations to accelerate conformity assessments ([European-Standards.com, 2026](https://european-standards.com/medical-devices-healthcare-equipment-standards/); [Measurlabs, 2026](https://measurlabs.com/blog/eu-mdr-medical-device-regulation/)).

- Restriction of Hazardous Substances (RoHS) and REACH: EU RoHS delegated directives revise certain lead exemptions taking effect in 2026; the EU has shifted scientific tasking to ECHA from 2027. China RoHS tightens in 2026; some countries require registration on specific platforms for RoHS SDoC. Suppliers should provide material declarations (e.g., IPC-1752A) and lab verification when needed; “trust but verify” is becoming enforcement norm ([UL Solutions, 2026](https://www.ul.com/news/global-market-access-regulatory-newsletter-q4-2025); [UL Solutions—RoHS comparison, 2025](https://www.ul.com/resources/global-market-access-regulatory-news-update); [Source Intelligence, 2026](https://blog.sourceintelligence.com/eu-rohs-compliance); [Certivo, 2026](https://www.certivo.com/blog-details/rohs-and-reach-compliance-lessons-from-2025-and-action-steps-for-2026)).

- Device submission pathways and implications for module suppliers:
  - U.S. 510(k): Typically 6–12 months; cost estimates vary from $50k–$500k in direct fees/consulting (excl. internal) to $1–$6 million total program cost depending on complexity—relevant if LGIT contemplates module or finished-device offerings rather than pure component supply ([AJProTech, 2026](https://ajprotech.com/blog/guides-and-templates/medical-devices-compliance-guide-cost-analysis-budget-strategies-and-fda-and-iso-requirements.html); [Complizen, 2025](https://www.complizen.ai/post/eu-mdr-vs-fda-complete-regulatory-comparison-guide-2025)).
  - U.S. PMA (Class III implantables like many neurostimulators): Multi-year and multi-million dollar clinical programs; under QMSR, supplier purchasing controls may be examined in more detail, including audit reports, increasing the compliance premium on implantable-component suppliers ([FDLI, 2025](https://www.fdli.org/2025/12/the-qmsr-transition-new-fda-guidance-and-their-impacts-on-active-implantable-medical-devices/)).
  - EU MDR CE-mark for Class IIb/III: 12–18 months and $0.5–$2.0 million typical for device makers; Notified Body capacity constraints persist. Module suppliers should align test evidence and material compliance to accelerate OEM submissions ([Complizen, 2025](https://www.complizen.ai/post/eu-mdr-vs-fda-complete-regulatory-comparison-guide-2025)).

B5. Time and cost to satisfy access requirements (component supplier perspective)

Degrandson notes manufacturers will “be asking their suppliers to get ISO Certification” although it is not a regulatory requirement; lead times for certification commonly span 6–12 months including gap closure and stage audits (industry norm; timing inferred). C8. Typical switching timelines (components/vendors)

- Change-control friction is high. Matric (electronics CM for med devices) emphasizes that if a PCB component approved in a 510(k) is changed, clearance is often required; even a $1 part can stall a $10,000 device for months. That implies vendor switches for critical electrodes/substrates—even at the component level—require extensive revalidation and may trigger regulatory submissions (e.g., design change notification, 510(k) supplement), making replacement timelines often 6–18 months end-to-end depending on class and risk ([Matric, 2026](https://www.matric.com/medical-device-supply-chain)).

- Supplier auditing cadence: Tier-1 suppliers are often audited prior to onboarding and every 1–2 years thereafter; Tier-2 every ~3 years; Tier-3 audits occur on cause. This cadence, plus device-level requalification, defines a realistic 9–18 month window to shift spend to a new supplier for critical electrodes/leads/substrates ([Greenlight Guru, 2025](https://www.greenlight.guru/blog/approved-supplier-list)).

Method
- We estimate SAM as the annual component revenue for “flexible substrate and electrode” content that LGIT could plausibly supply in 2026 for initial regions (North America + Europe), then globally.

D4. Entry level choice: component vs module vs integrated system

- Component/module focus is favored:
  - Regulatory burden: Finished-device paths (510(k), PMA, EU MDR CE-mark) are 6–18+ months and $0.5–$5+ million+; LGIT currently lacks a medical market footprint. Component/module offerings can monetize core competencies sooner by plugging into OEM/CDMO programs with ASL/AVL onboarding and module-level testing (EMC/safety for active subassemblies) ([Complizen, 2025](https://www.complizen.ai/post/eu-mdr-vs-fda-complete-regulatory-comparison-guide-2025); [AJProTech, 2026](https://ajprotech.com/blog/guides-and-templates/medical-devices-compliance-guide-cost-analysis-budget-strategies-and-fda-and-iso-requirements.html)).

  - Defensible differentiation at substrate/electrode layer: LGIT’s 10 µm lithography, fine-line traces, biocompatible thin films, and R2R scale directly map to performance drivers in CGM electrodes (sensitivity/selectivity, longevity), neurostim leads (impedance, channel density, reliability), and flexible neural probes (Au/Pt/PEDOT:PSS on parylene/PI). Academic and precommercial systems highlight the need for such stacks (PEDOT:PSS impedance reduction by ~23-fold while preserving microelectrode size) ([Microsystems & Nanoengineering, 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41378-024-00685-6); [RSC Analyst, 2022](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlelanding/2022/an/d1an02214h)).

  - Procurement practicality: OEMs/CDMOs prefer fewer “box-build” changes; module-level offerings can be attractive if they do not reclassify the OEM’s submission substantially. But even module swapping can trigger submissions; starting with component-level (electrodes, flexible interposers) often reduces change-control complexity and speeds qualification ([Matric, 2026](https://www.matric.com/medical-device-supply-chain)).

E. Route-to-Market Execution Plan and Reachability

E1. 12–24 month qualification pathway (aligned to QMSR/MDR supplier controls)

- Months 0–3: QMS gap assessment to ISO 13485 with FDA QMSR overlays (ensure alignment with 21 CFR 803, 806, 821, 830; define supplier files, design controls where applicable to modules); launch supplier-quality and document-control systems. Prepare audit readiness (mock audits per Veranex approach) ([Greenlight Guru, 2026](https://www.greenlight.guru/blog/qmsr-your-guide-to-part-820); [Veranex, 2026](https://veranex.com/medical-device-quality-consulting/medical-device-audit-services)).

- Months 2–6: Establish ASL/AVL onboarding package: supplier questionnaire, quality manual, process flows, special processes (e.g., lithography, metallization), capability studies; adopt audit clause language allowing Notified Body access; create biocompatibility master file (ISO 10993) and RoHS/REACH materials dossiers with lab verification for high-risk items ([Greenlight Guru, 2025](https://www.greenlight.guru/blog/approved-supplier-list); [Measurlabs, 2026](https://measurlabs.com/blog/eu-mdr-medical-device-regulation/); [Source Intelligence, 2026](https://blog.sourceintelligence.com/eu-rohs-compliance)).

- Months 4–12: Pursue Tier-1 audits with a lead CDMO and one CGM OEM; run EVT/DVT builds for electrode arrays and flexible PCBs; agree to change-control templates modeled on FDA/MDR expectations (documented rationales for changes, risk analysis per ISO 14971, validation plans) ([FDA, 2024](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/02/02/2024-01709/medical-devices-quality-system-regulation-amendments)).

- Months 9–18: Convert first CGM patch electrode/FPCB program to production ramp; expand to a second customer. Begin implantable-lead/paddle-electrode prototypes with an implantable OEM or Integer-type CDMO (expect multi-quarter bench, preclinical, and reliability testing prior to transfer).

  - Neuro/BCI: Parylene C with Au/Pt layers and PEDOT:PSS electrodeposition for impedance reduction; flexible shanks and high-density arrays—all play to LGIT’s microfabrication strengths ([Microsystems & Nanoengineering, 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41378-024-00685-6)).

G. Risks and Mitigations

- Regulatory switching risk: Replacement components may trigger re-submissions (510(k) supplements) or extended validations—lengthening sales cycles. Mitigation: early co-development, drop-in compatibility studies, and robust change-control documentation reduce approval risk ([Matric, 2026](https://www.matric.com/medical-device-supply-chain)).

- Audit access reluctance: Some electronics suppliers resist NB audit clauses; MDR ecosystems expect them. Mitigation: pre-approved quality agreement templates with audit access, backed by ISO 13485 and internal audit logs, increase acceptability ([Greenlight Guru, 2025](https://www.greenlight.guru/blog/approved-supplier-list)).

- Material compliance drift: RoHS/REACH updates can invalidate previously acceptable materials. Mitigation: “trust, but verify” with lab tests for high-risk components; maintain a compliance BOM and supplier-declaration surveillance to prevent customs holds ([Certivo, 2026](https://www.certivo.com/blog-details/rohs-and-reach-compliance-lessons-from-2025-and-action-steps-for-2026)).

- Capacity and scale credibility: Medical OEMs value continuity and redundancy. Mitigation: demonstrate R2R capability, second-source plans, and alignment with CDMOs for overflow; leverage ISO 13485 and mock audits to signal audit-readiness ([Veranex, 2026](https://veranex.com/medical-device-quality-consulting/medical-device-audit-services)).

H. Conclusion and Recommendation

LG Innotek’s substrate-level competencies are directly monetizable and defensible in CGM and neuro/BCI components where thin-film electrodes and flexible interconnects determine clinical performance and user experience. The dominant route-to-market is direct enterprise sales into OEM purchasing with parallel CDMO partnerships; marketplaces play no material role for critical, patient-contact components. The key gates are ISO 13485–aligned QMS readiness under QMSR, acceptance of audit rights (MDR/NBOG), and comprehensive materials/biocompatibility documentation.

We estimate an initial (2026) NA+EU SAM of $2.1–$3.3B and a global SAM of $2.8–$4.0B for flexible substrates and electrodes in CGM, neurostimulation, and emergent BCI niches. A realistic 24–36 month SOM is $25–45M (0.5–1.0% share), scaling to $80–130M in five years (2–4% share) with two anchor OEM wins and one CDMO master service agreement. The risk-adjusted path is to prioritize component/module supply—especially CGM electrodes/FPCBs—over launching finished devices, to shorten time to revenue and avoid large regulatory capex before establishing a medical track record.
