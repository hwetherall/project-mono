Title: Structural Trends, Growth Quality, and Downside Scenarios for Advanced Flexible Substrates and Electrode Modules in MedTech (CGM, Neurostimulation, BCI)

Executive summary

This report analyzes structural opportunity and threat trends, macro context, and downside scenarios for suppliers of advanced medical device substrates, flexible circuit components, and electrode modules serving continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), neurostimulation, and brain-computer interface (BCI) markets in North America and Europe (with a view to global expansion). It is written to inform a market-entry strategy for LG Innotek (LGIT), which has deep competencies in chip-on-flex (CoF) and flexible PCB (FPCB) manufacturing at ~10 μm-class resolution in IT and automotive but lacks medical certifications, biosignal IP, and finished-device experience.

Key findings

- Opportunity trends are durable and demand-creating. They include: (1) a shift to adaptive/closed-loop neuromodulation and BCIs, increasing the value of high-density flexible electrodes and implant-grade substrates; (2) regulatory convergence (FDA QMSR aligning to ISO 13485) and intensifying postmarket enforcement, which elevates qualified component/CDMO partners; (3) compliance digitization anchored on Full Material Declarations (FMDs), SCIP/Digital Product Passports, and AI-enabled supplier hubs—favoring data-ready, design-integrated suppliers; (4) supply-chain regionalization (EU/UK/EU MDR reforms and tariff/trade volatility) driving pre-validated alternates and local capacity; and (5) sustained capital formation and clinical progress in invasive neurotech programs, creating concentrated but meaningful demand for implant-grade electrode arrays and flexible hybrid electronics (FHE) assemblies ([Cirtec](https://www.cirtecmed.com/solutions/brain-computer-interface-bci); [FDA QMSR](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/postmarket-requirements-devices/quality-management-system-regulation-qmsr); [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/03/what-medtech-companies-can-expect-in-2026-regulation-risk-and-resilience); [LinkedIn—Nelson](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/medical-device-supply-chain-compliance-2026-turning-advantage-nelson-9oxoe); [MedWorld Advisors](https://www.medworldadvisors.com/blog/tariffs-and-the-medtech-industry-disruption-realignment-and-strategic-consequences); [New Market Pitch—Funding Trends](https://newmarketpitch.com/blogs/news/neurotech-funding-trends)).

- Threat trends are also structural. They include: (1) tariff-driven cost inflation and “tariff stacking” across subcomponents, tied to a high import share of medical device components; (2) PFAS restrictions and hazardous substance controls triggering component obsolescence and redesign risk; (3) increased FDA enforcement based on postmarket signal mining; (4) commoditization pressures in flexible circuits with entrenched medtech CDMOs and precision interconnect suppliers; and (5) semiconductor supply/packaging constraints that can ripple into device programs using custom ASICs ([MedWorld Advisors](https://www.medworldadvisors.com/blog/tariffs-and-the-medtech-industry-disruption-realignment-and-strategic-consequences); [LinkedIn—Nelson](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/medical-device-supply-chain-compliance-2026-turning-advantage-nelson-9oxoe); [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/03/what-medtech-companies-can-expect-in-2026-regulation-risk-and-resilience); [TechPowerUp—imec/PDKs](https://www.techpowerup.com/news-archive)).

- Buyer behavior is observably changing. Top device OEMs are deepening outsourcing to med CDMOs with ISO 13485 and FDA-registered facilities, while revising coding/economic models for integrated neurostim systems—evidenced by 2024 CPT changes distinguishing integrated vs. traditional systems. Concurrently, compliance functions are “inverting” supplier engagement—minimizing ad hoc attestations by ingesting structured FMD/BOM data and aligning outreach to supplier business cycles, which rewards suppliers that curate compliance-ready data and sub-tier mapping ([Flex](https://flex.com/industries/healthcare); [ASRA—Coding](https://asra.com/news-publications/asra-newsletter/newsletter-item/asra-news/2024/08/08/changes-in-coding-and-payment-for-neuromodulation-procedures-in-2024); [LinkedIn—Nelson](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/medical-device-supply-chain-compliance-2026-turning-advantage-nelson-9oxoe)).

- Growth quality is mixed but net structural. Neurostimulation shows steady demand with US DBS up approximately +11% YoY in 2025, while SCS was closer to +3% amid payer scrutiny; invasive neurotech funding remains concentrated but large (e.g., $650M Neuralink round in 2025), suggesting fewer, larger programs driving subcomponent volume. Medtech overall remains a large, growing sector, with global device markets estimated at $572–$679B in 2025 and projected to exceed $1T by the mid-2030s, implying durable multi-year growth independent of single-cycle demand ([SmartTRAK](https://blog.smarttrak.com/all); [New Market Pitch—Funding Deals](https://newmarketpitch.com/blogs/news/neurotech-funding-deals); [MedTech Intelligence](https://medtechintelligence.com/feature_article/the-510k-pathway-in-2026-navigating-a-shifting-regulatory-and-political-landscape-for-medical-devices/)).

- Market-entry recommendation for LG Innotek: A three-beachhead B2B strategy aligned to certifications and risk.
  1) Wearable CGM substrate modules (non-sterile or semi-critical components): Chip-on-flex sensor interposers, ultra-thin FPC tails, and high-reliability interconnects that meet biocompatible exposure constraints—delivered under ISO 13485-centric QMS with FMD-backed BOMs integrated into OEM PLM workflows. This leverages LGIT’s precision FPC/CoF processes with the lowest regulatory friction and the largest repeat volume opportunities ([Flex](https://flex.com/industries/healthcare); [FDA 510(k)](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/premarket-submissions-selecting-and-preparing-correct-submission/premarket-notification-510k)).
  2) Implantable neurostimulation interconnect and electrode substrates (sub-assemblies, not finished implants): Rigid-flex implantable PCBs, passive-embedded FPCs for IPGs/leads, and thin-film electrode carriers (in partnership with established implant suppliers) with validated reliability under long-term mechanical and fluid exposure as emphasized by neuro OEMs and CDMOs ([Cirtec](https://www.cirtecmed.com/solutions/brain-computer-interface-bci); [DYCONEX](https://dyconex.com/)).
  3) BCI electrode arrays (thin-film arrays and FHE sub-modules) in joint development with clinical-stage BCI developers: Focus on manufacturability and chronic reliability with early-stage volumes, co-validating performance under strain/thermal cycling/fluids, building toward scale as pivotal trials succeed ([Cirtec](https://www.cirtecmed.com/solutions/brain-computer-interface-bci)).

Each beachhead requires staged certification (ISO 13485, FDA registration where appropriate), advanced supplier data functions (FMDs/SCIP), and regional capacity to mitigate tariffs and recertification cycles in EU/UK. Early partnerships with leading CDMOs and precision interconnect houses will accelerate qualification and unlock OEM funnels, while building LGIT’s medical credibility without taking full device regulatory risk ([FDA QMSR](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/postmarket-requirements-devices/quality-management-system-regulation-qmsr); [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/03/what-medtech-companies-can-expect-in-2026-regulation-risk-and-resilience); [LinkedIn—Nelson](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/medical-device-supply-chain-compliance-2026-turning-advantage-nelson-9oxoe)).

Scope and definitions

- Industry focus: Medical devices/MedTech addressing CGM, neurostimulation (including SCS, PNS, cranial nerve stimulation), and BCI implants and accessories.
- Solution category: Advanced medical device substrates (FPCB, CoF), flexible hybrid electronics (FHE) sub-modules, and electrode arrays/modules for wearable and implantable use.
- Geography: North America and Europe as initial target markets; global as expansion.
- Business model: B2B component supply and CDMO-like development/manufacturing services.
- Target buyer: Enterprise OEM medical device manufacturers and their CDMO partners.

Top structural threat trends (with mechanisms, persistence, evidence)

1) Tariffs and “tariff stacking” inflate BOM costs and disrupt supply continuity.

- Mechanism: Multiple duties apply cumulatively at subcomponent and finished-device levels; many US-made devices still rely on 50–80% imported components, making domestic assembly exposed to import cost inflation and volatility. OEMs seek reshoring and diversified supply bases; smaller firms with less margin compress or become acquisition targets ([MedWorld Advisors](https://www.medworldadvisors.com/blog/tariffs-and-the-medtech-industry-disruption-realignment-and-strategic-consequences)). Persistence: Structural, driven by geopolitical cycles and policy shifts rather than temporary shocks. Signals: Panel discussions at MD&M West; reintroduced retaliatory tariffs in EU on certain US medical-use products; OEM strategic chatter on rebalancing supply chains ([MedWorld Advisors](https://www.medworldadvisors.com/blog/tariffs-and-the-medtech-industry-disruption-realignment-and-strategic-consequences)).

2) PFAS restrictions and hazardous substance rules trigger obsolescence and redesign cycles.

- Mechanism: Phaseouts and SCIP submissions force proactive engineering changes and nested BOM transparency; suppliers without FMD-backed chain-of-custody or material alternates cause delays or revalidation costs. PFAS shifted from reporting to material availability crisis in 2025, highlighting redesign risks ([LinkedIn—Nelson](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/medical-device-supply-chain-compliance-2026-turning-advantage-nelson-9oxoe)). Persistence: Structural across the decade as Digital Product Passports and sustainability reporting mature. Signals: Programs building sub-tier mapping grounded in FMDs and integrating sustainability with compliance operations ([LinkedIn—Nelson](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/medical-device-supply-chain-compliance-2026-turning-advantage-nelson-9oxoe)).

3) Intensified FDA enforcement based on postmarket signal mining and QMSR-based risk integration.

- Mechanism: With MDSAP reducing duplicative audits, FDA reallocates to investigating firms tied to patient-safety signals, requiring stronger quality culture, risk management integration, and postmarket responsiveness. This raises the selection bar for component suppliers and CDMOs aligned to QMSR and ISO 13485 practices ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/03/what-medtech-companies-can-expect-in-2026-regulation-risk-and-resilience)). Persistence: Structural; QMSR effective February 2, 2026. Signals: FDA communications and law-firm insights on QMSR deployment and enforcement increases ([FDA QMSR](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/postmarket-requirements-devices/quality-management-system-regulation-qmsr); [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/03/what-medtech-companies-can-expect-in-2026-regulation-risk-and-resilience)).

4) Commoditization pressure from entrenched medtech CDMOs and precision interconnect specialists.

- Mechanism: Established players with ISO 13485 footprints, Class III specialization, and med-specific V&V compress margins for generic flexible circuits. Differentiation requires chronic implant reliability, miniaturized HDI/HF capabilities, integrated passives, and compliance data pipelines. Competitors include CDMOs (Flex, Celestica/Benchmark) and precision PCB makers (DYCONEX) with implantable credentials ([Flex](https://flex.com/industries/healthcare); [DYCONEX](https://dyconex.com/)). Persistence: Structural; entry barriers are quality, validation, and relationships, not transient pricing.

5) Semiconductor and packaging capacity constraints ripple into custom ICs and modules.

- Mechanism: High-NA EUV transitions and constrained advanced nodes extend lead times and raise costs for ASICs and advanced packaging used in some next-gen neuromod/BCI systems. While many implants run on mature nodes, bleeding-edge modules (e.g., high-channel-count amplifiers, advanced packaging) are exposed. European initiatives (NanoIC PDKs for fine-pitch RDL/hybrid bonding) show the system response but underscore dependency ([TechPowerUp—imec High-NA](https://www.techpowerup.com/news-archive); [TechPowerUp—NanoIC PDKs](https://www.techpowerup.com/news-archive)). Persistence: Structural through next process nodes; cyclic with capex cycles.

Trend-to-mechanism evidence and persistence

- Closed-loop/adaptive neuromod and BCI development explicitly demand electrode arrays and flexible hybrid integration that withstand mechanical strain, thermal cycling, and long-term fluid exposure—accelerating demand for implant-grade flexible substrates and thin-film electrode assemblies ([Cirtec](https://www.cirtecmed.com/solutions/brain-computer-interface-bci)). This is a multi-year structural trend embedded in OEM R&D roadmaps and CDMO portfolios.

- QMSR harmonization to ISO 13485 is codified (effective February 2, 2026). FDA and legal advisories emphasize integrated risk management and “quality culture,” increasing diligence on sub-suppliers. This is structural and alters supplier qualification criteria across the decade ([FDA QMSR](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/postmarket-requirements-devices/quality-management-system-regulation-qmsr); [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/03/what-medtech-companies-can-expect-in-2026-regulation-risk-and-resilience)).

- Compliance digitization with FMDs, Supplier Hubs, and AI ingestion is reported to increase evidence quality and reduce cycle times by aligning outreach cadence with supplier business cycles—this is operationalized into programs and persists beyond news cycles ([LinkedIn—Nelson](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/medical-device-supply-chain-compliance-2026-turning-advantage-nelson-9oxoe)).

- EU/UK reforms (UK’s recertification and potential international reliance framework) plus EUDAMED transparency shift emphasize cross-jurisdictional diligence and pre-validation. These are structural regulatory transitions with multi-year timelines impacting transaction planning and market access ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/03/what-medtech-companies-can-expect-in-2026-regulation-risk-and-resilience)).

- Tariff/trade volatility has already prompted reshoring, diversification, and M&A to localize production for US access, indicating structural strategy changes rather than temporary price shocks ([MedWorld Advisors](https://www.medworldadvisors.com/blog/tariffs-and-the-medtech-industry-disruption-realignment-and-strategic-consequences)).

B. Macro context shaping this market

Macro drivers and causal chains

- Regulatory harmonization and enforcement intensity (QMSR replacing QSR): Forces OEMs to re-assess supplier quality systems, risk management integration, and postmarket surveillance responsiveness. Buyers favor suppliers with ISO 13485-ready QMS, MDSAP alignment, and demonstrable “quality culture” (empowered personnel, proactive CAPA). Result: higher barriers to entry but competitive advantage for compliant suppliers ([FDA QMSR](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/postmarket-requirements-devices/quality-management-system-regulation-qmsr); [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/03/what-medtech-companies-can-expect-in-2026-regulation-risk-and-resilience)).

- Compliance digitization and AI adoption: Buyers embed compliance into design decisions via FMD-supported BOMs integrated into PLM; AI automates supplier data ingestion/validation and predictive risk modeling. Supplier Hubs consolidate engagement, reducing fatigue and improving evidence quality. Result: suppliers are selected for their data readiness and integration capabilities—beyond component cost/performance ([LinkedIn—Nelson](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/medical-device-supply-chain-compliance-2026-turning-advantage-nelson-9oxoe)).

- Tariffs/trade and national security: Global supply chains face recurring shocks; OEMs diversify geography and pre-validate alternates down to part/substance levels to mitigate redesign risk and tariff volatility. Result: premium accrues to regional, certified capacity with transparent sub-tier mapping and sustainability alignment ([MedWorld Advisors](https://www.medworldadvisors.com/blog/tariffs-and-the-medtech-industry-disruption-realignment-and-strategic-consequences); [LinkedIn—Nelson](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/medical-device-supply-chain-compliance-2026-turning-advantage-nelson-9oxoe)).

- EU/UK regulatory transitions and reliance frameworks: All devices must be recertified under new UK rules; potential reliance on comparator regulators (AU, CA, EU, US) can streamline approvals but requires cross-jurisdictional mapping. Result: OEMs seek suppliers fluent in multi-region compliance and data transparency to protect valuations and timelines ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/03/what-medtech-companies-can-expect-in-2026-regulation-risk-and-resilience)).

- Evolving FDA posture toward consumer digital health: Certain consumer products may be viewed as unregulated general wellness devices. For component suppliers, this expands the market for wearable, non-implant biosensor modules, while implants remain under stringent device frameworks. Result: dual-track opportunities in wearable and implantable modules ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/03/what-medtech-companies-can-expect-in-2026-regulation-risk-and-resilience)).

Observable buyer behavior changes

- Outsourcing to med-specialized CDMOs: OEMs lean on CDMOs with robust quality footprints, global sites, and advanced manufacturing capabilities (automation, robotics, simulation) to meet time-to-market and compliance demands. Flex’s 21 ISO 13485 sites and 14 FDA-certified sites illustrate this scale, along with a broad portfolio from imaging systems to drug delivery and surgical robotics ([Flex](https://flex.com/industries/healthcare)).

- Integration of compliance into design controls: Programs that normalize supplier outreach cadence, use FMDs for sub-tier mapping, and align sustainability with compliance operations report better response rates, evidence quality, and cycle times—indicating buyers reward suppliers who minimize friction and deliver structured data integrated with PLM ([LinkedIn—Nelson](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/medical-device-supply-chain-compliance-2026-turning-advantage-nelson-9oxoe)).

- Coding/payment evolution favoring integrated neurostimulation: The 2024 CPT updates distinguished traditional vs. integrated systems (with new Category III programming codes for integrated SCS systems and new sacral/tibial integrated codes), suggesting physician workflows and revenue cycles are adapting to integrated modules—supporting adoption of electrode+stim integration strategies ([ASRA](https://asra.com/news-publications/asra-newsletter/newsletter-item/asra-news/2024/08/08/changes-in-coding-and-payment-for-neuromodulation-procedures-in-2024)).

- Regulatory diligence in dealmaking: Buyers evaluate pathway timing, inspection history, recalls, and responsiveness to regulatory feedback more intensely; this extends to supplier qualification and sub-tier visibility in M&A and platform integration planning ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/03/what-medtech-companies-can-expect-in-2026-regulation-risk-and-resilience)).

C. Growth quality and downside

Structural versus cyclical demand indicators

- Neurostimulation: US Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS) market revenues in 2025 were up ~+11% YoY; Spinal Cord Stimulation (SCS) grew ~+3% with payer scrutiny, indicating persistent but modality-specific growth. This suggests structural need with some cyclical reimbursement pressure ([SmartTRAK](https://blog.smarttrak.com/all)).

- Invasive neurotech capital: 2025 saw extreme deal concentration (e.g., $650M Neuralink round), with invasive BCIs capturing the largest funding share. Non-invasive systems raised smaller rounds on average. The trend is structural in ambitions and timelines but cyclical in deal counts and concentration—implying concentrated buyer power for implant-grade components ([New Market Pitch—Funding Deals](https://newmarketpitch.com/blogs/news/neurotech-funding-deals)).

- Industry scale and trajectory: Global medical device markets were estimated at $572–$679B in 2025 and are projected to exceed $1T by the mid-2030s, reflecting multi-year structural drivers beyond single-year budget cycles ([MedTech Intelligence](https://medtechintelligence.com/feature_article/the-510k-pathway-in-2026-navigating-a-shifting-regulatory-and-political-landscape-for-medical-devices/)).

- Compliance and regulatory transformation: QMSR alignment to ISO 13485 and intensified postmarket enforcement are structural—altering supplier selection and ongoing qualification, not subject to short-term swings ([FDA QMSR](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/postmarket-requirements-devices/quality-management-system-regulation-qmsr); [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/03/what-medtech-companies-can-expect-in-2026-regulation-risk-and-resilience)).

Named downside scenarios

1) Tariff escalation and “stacking” on critical subcomponents.

- Scenario: New or expanded tariffs on medical-use polymers, specialty metals, and electronic subassemblies applied at multiple tiers (components and finished devices) drive BOM inflation and reorder OEM sourcing. Mechanism: Accretive duties on imported subcomponents raise costs for US-assembled devices, with 50–80% of components in US-made devices imported; OEMs accelerate reshoring and pre-validated alternates, potentially delaying new supplier awards. Evidence: Reports of tariff stacking and high import shares in US-made devices; supply chain rebalancing and M&A to localize production ([MedWorld Advisors](https://www.medworldadvisors.com/blog/tariffs-and-the-medtech-industry-disruption-realignment-and-strategic-consequences)). Risk to LGIT: Margin compression and qualification delays if capacity is Asia-centric and not regionally diversified.

2) PFAS and hazardous substance regulatory tightening triggers redesign cycles.

- Scenario: Regional bans or unacceptable concentrations discovered in sub-tier materials (e.g., lubricants, coatings) in electrode or substrate stacks necessitate rapid engineering change orders (ECOs), revalidation, and potential product holds. Mechanism: Programs with FMD-based nested BOMs fare better; suppliers relying on attestations face longer cycles and lower evidence quality. Evidence: 2025 saw PFAS restrictions evolve into material availability crises; best programs anchor on FMDs and cadence-aligned supplier engagement ([LinkedIn—Nelson](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/medical-device-supply-chain-compliance-2026-turning-advantage-nelson-9oxoe)). Risk to LGIT: Reduced win probability if FMD/SCIP data not embedded in offering.

3) FDA postmarket signal-triggered enforcement increases supplier scrutiny.

- Scenario: OEM products tied to safety signals prompt FDA information requests and inspections domestically and abroad; component suppliers without robust QMS, risk management, and documentation become liabilities; OEMs switch to suppliers with better compliance posture. Mechanism: MDSAP participation frees FDA for safety-focused enforcement; QMSR emphasizes risk management and quality culture. Evidence: Law-firm analysis on enforcement priorities, risk integration, and diligence implications ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/03/what-medtech-companies-can-expect-in-2026-regulation-risk-and-resilience)). Risk to LGIT: Disqualification risk without ISO 13485/QMSR mapping and strong CAPA systems.

4) Funding drought or consolidation among BCI/implant pioneers reduces supplier funnel.

- Scenario: Concentrated venture funding leads to fewer platform winners; delays in pivotal trials or reimbursement slowdowns reduce volumes and shutter smaller programs; suppliers overexposed to early-stage BCIs face demand volatility. Mechanism: 2025 funding showed high concentration in a few invasive BCIs; deal counts fluctuate; regulatory and reimbursement pathways remain evolving. Evidence: Funding concentration, deal-count volatility in 2024–2025; warnings about enduring 510(k) flaws and safety crises impacting market confidence ([New Market Pitch—Funding Deals](https://newmarketpitch.com/blogs/news/neurotech-funding-deals); [MedTech Intelligence](https://medtechintelligence.com/feature_article/the-510k-pathway-in-2026-navigating-a-shifting-regulatory-and-political-landscape-for-medical-devices/)). Risk to LGIT: Portfolio exposure to a small set of BCIs may drive uneven capacity utilization.

5) Advanced packaging/ASIC supply shocks elongate development cycles for high-channel systems.

- Scenario: Tight advanced-node capacity (or packaging transitions) delay custom ASIC deliveries for neuromod/BCI, causing program reschedules and supplier re-qualification. Mechanism: Industry constraints at sub-2 nm/high-NA EUV stages; early-access PDKs for advanced interconnect/hybrid bonding indicate a catching-up phase. Evidence: Industry reports of N2 node constraints and premium “hot lots”; imec/NanoIC pushing PDKs; High NA EUV installation milestones show ongoing transition risk ([TechPowerUp—news archive](https://www.techpowerup.com/news-archive)). Risk to LGIT: Indirect schedule impacts; opportunity to offer design-for-manufacturability (DFM) alternatives and passive-embedded substrates while ASICs lag.

Historical slowdown analogues

- Neuromodulation market post-COVID: SmartTRAK reported that by Q1 2023, neuromodulation growth was resuming, with manufacturers noting improved staffing and post-COVID demand. This implies a 2020–2022 period of muted growth tied to pandemic disruptions in elective procedures and hospital throughput—demonstrating how exogenous shocks can disrupt demand for implantable devices and associated components, with a ~2–3 year normalization window ([SmartTRAK](https://blog.smarttrak.com/all)).

- Safety crises and regulatory reappraisal effects: The transvaginal mesh debacle, Philips Respironics recall, and Penumbra JET 7 have been cited as evidence that the US device safety architecture lagged sector complexity, fueling calls for reform and increased diligence. These events can trigger market pullbacks, revisions of evidence standards, and more conservative buyer behavior—slowing new platform adoption and extending validation cycles ([MedTech Intelligence](https://medtechintelligence.com/feature_article/the-510k-pathway-in-2026-navigating-a-shifting-regulatory-and-political-landscape-for-medical-devices/)).

- Reimbursement and coding changes: In 2006, CMS adjustments demonstrated that reimbursement can swing materially for neurodiagnostics and stimulators—MEG reimbursements were cut significantly before partial restoration, while implanted neurostimulators saw increases and new-technology add-ons in certain settings. Such shifts reshape economic viability of therapies, influencing adoption and procurement of associated components ([Neurotech Reports](https://neurotechreports.com/pages/reimbursement06.html)).

Market-entry blueprint for LG Innotek

Position to win: Where LGIT can defensibly participate

Given LGIT’s strength in CoF/FPC with ~10 μm-class resolution (as inferred from advanced substrate communications) and sustainability innovation (e.g., ECONOVA eliminating Au/Pd processing in advanced substrates), the best defensible positions are in high-density flexible substrate modules and electrode carriers where manufacturing precision, yield, and compliance-data robustness are key differentiators. Avoid full finished-device exposure until med certifications and clinical/regulatory capabilities mature.

- Beachhead 1: Wearable CGM flexible substrate modules
  - What to supply: Ultra-thin flexible interconnects, CoF-based sensor interposers, tail assemblies, and sealed transitions designed for skin-contact devices, with controlled biocompatibility on exposed surfaces and robust ingress protection. Build for scale, consistent adhesion/lamination, and low-profile, high-yield bonding.
  - Why defensible: High volume, precision FPC/CoF manufacturing is core LGIT strength; regulatory burden is lower than implants; OEM buyers value suppliers that integrate FMD-backed BOMs and AI-enabled compliance ingestion into PLM workflows to minimize redesign and obsolescence risk ([LinkedIn—Nelson](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/medical-device-supply-chain-compliance-2026-turning-advantage-nelson-9oxoe); [FDA 510(k)](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/premarket-submissions-selecting-and-preparing-correct-submission/premarket-notification-510k)).
  - How to enter: Qualify as second source through incumbent CM partners (e.g., Flex) and directly with OEM engineering teams; offer DFM to reduce scrap and assembly time. Emphasize data transparency (FMD/SCIP) and sustainability alignment (precious-metal elimination when feasible in non-critical traces) to support EU-focused supply chains ([Flex](https://flex.com/industries/healthcare); [LG Innotek—ECONOVA post](https://www.linkedin.com/company/lg-innotek)).

- Beachhead 2: Implantable neurostimulation interconnects and electrode substrates (sub-assemblies)
  - What to supply: Implantable rigid-flex circuits for IPG/lead interconnects; thin-film electrode carriers and substrates for cuff, paddle, or strip electrodes in partnership with electrode specialists; high-reliability feedthrough interposers (non-hermetic components supplied to OEM hermetic-packaging partners).
  - Why defensible: Demand for high-density, flexible substrates with chronic reliability under strain/thermal cycles/fluids is explicitly cited by neuromod/BCI CDMOs; LGIT’s precision processes and ability to embed passives could enable integrated modules aligned with coding and programming shifts toward integrated systems ([Cirtec](https://www.cirtecmed.com/solutions/brain-computer-interface-bci); [ASRA](https://asra.com/news-publications/asra-newsletter/newsletter-item/asra-news/2024/08/08/changes-in-coding-and-payment-for-neuromodulation-procedures-in-2024)).
  - How to enter: Obtain ISO 13485 and map QMSR; co-develop verification/validation protocols for chronic conditions with partners; demonstrate mechanical and electrochemical stability; deliver comprehensive design-history and postmarket support documentation to OEMs and CDMOs ([FDA QMSR](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/postmarket-requirements-devices/quality-management-system-regulation-qmsr)).

- Beachhead 3: BCI thin-film arrays and flexible hybrid modules (pilot-stage)
  - What to supply: Custom electrode arrays on biocompatible flexible substrates with integrated routing, shielding, and interconnects; FHE sub-modules pairing arrays with ASIC interposers; high-channel-density interposers using fine-line/space RDL analogs primed for manufacturability and reliability.
  - Why defensible: Early supplier involvement in clinical-stage BCIs enables co-validation and long-term lock-in; LGIT can differentiate on manufacturability, chronic reliability, and compliance data curation—critical as platforms approach pivotal trials ([Cirtec](https://www.cirtecmed.com/solutions/brain-computer-interface-bci); [New Market Pitch—Funding Deals](https://newmarketpitch.com/blogs/news/neurotech-funding-deals)).

Risk mitigations aligned to downside scenarios

- Tariffs: Build dual-region manufacturing (NA/EU), maintain country-of-origin flexibility, and design multi-source-able materials stacks to minimize duty exposure; include tariff sensitivity in quotes to protect margins ([MedWorld Advisors](https://www.medworldadvisors.com/blog/tariffs-and-the-medtech-industry-disruption-realignment-and-strategic-consequences)).

- PFAS/hazardous substances: Maintain alternate qualified materials and process recipes; keep FMDs current and integrable into OEM PLM; pilot DPP integration early with EU customers ([LinkedIn—Nelson](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/medical-device-supply-chain-compliance-2026-turning-advantage-nelson-9oxoe)).

- Enforcement/QMSR: Invest in internal audit functions tied to ISO 13485 and QMSR gap analyses; ensure traceability down to sub-tier; pre-build CAPA playbooks for implant-related nonconformities ([FDA QMSR](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/postmarket-requirements-devices/quality-management-system-regulation-qmsr)).

- BCI concentration: Diversify across neurostimulation modalities (SCS/PNS/cranial nerve) and wearable CGM modules; phase capital tied to BCI volumes; seek milestone-based co-development structures ([New Market Pitch—Funding Deals](https://newmarketpitch.com/blogs/news/neurotech-funding-deals)).

- ASIC/package constraints: Engage early with OEM silicon partners; design for mature-node compatibility where feasible; propose passive-embedded substrate features to reduce ASIC I/O count or complexity; monitor European PDK developments for potential alternative packaging flows ([TechPowerUp—NanoIC PDKs](https://www.techpowerup.com/news-archive)).

My view on growth quality and strategic fit

The substrate/electrode module layer in CGM, neurostimulation, and BCI sits at the intersection of durable demand and rising compliance/selectivity. The strongest tailwinds—closed-loop/BCI complexity, QMSR/ISO 13485 harmonization, and compliance digitization—are structural and advantage suppliers who can: (1) demonstrate chronic reliability in implant environments, (2) deliver integration-ready compliance data, and (3) provide regional, resilient capacity.

LGIT’s core strengths in high-density flexible substrates and CoF, plus demonstrable sustainability engineering, map well to CGM and neurostimulation interconnect/electrode substrates. The implantable BCI array space offers attractive lock-in and differentiation but requires staged capability building and co-validation with clinical partners. The most defensible near-term play is to become a compliance-first, regionally diversified component supplier for wearable CGM and implantable neuro interconnects—partnering closely with leading med CDMOs to accelerate qualification—while pursuing selective BCI JDs to build implant-grade credibility without overexposure to funding concentration risk.
