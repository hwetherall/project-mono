**Research Report: External Forces Creating Urgency for U.S. Municipal Water Utilities to Address Leakage and Non-Revenue Water**

*Prepared: 10 March 2026*  

---

## Abstract
Municipal water systems in North America lose an estimated 20-30 % of treated drinking water before it reaches customers, translating into roughly US $2.6 billion in annual non-revenue water (NRW) losses. This report synthesizes recent regulatory, technological, economic, demographic, and competitive forces that are accelerating utility action on the problem, with special focus on mid-sized U.S. utilities serving 50 000-500 000 residents. Five major forcing-function clusters are identified: (1) hard regulatory deadlines (EPA Lead & Copper Rule Improvements, California SB 555, Canada’s proposed Safe Water Infrastructure Act), (2) technology sunsets and digital funding incentives, (3) escalating leak-related costs and water scarcity, (4) demographic shifts within the water workforce, and (5) budget-cycle catalysts linked to record federal and state infrastructure grants. Trend data show leak frequency, repair costs, and regulatory citations rising 3-9 % per year since 2017. Trigger events such as high-profile main breaks, annual water loss audits, and rate-case filings reliably jolt utilities into procurement mode. Fiscal budgeting analyses indicate primary decision windows between September and March, aligning with the start of most municipal fiscal years (July 1 or October 1). Overall urgency is assessed as **high**, peaking in 2026-2028 as the first wave of federal enforcement and bond-funding spend-down deadlines converge with mass retirements of senior operators. Mid-sized utilities in water-stressed U.S. Sun Belt and West Coast states feel the pressure most acutely.

---

## 1. Introduction
North American drinking-water utilities are grappling with a convergence of deteriorating assets, intensifying regulatory scrutiny, workforce attrition, and customer intolerance for service disruptions. Traditionally, leak detection relied on labor-intensive “listening” surveys covering just a few pipe-miles per day, making proactive management all but impossible. Over the past three years, numerous external forces have turned what was once a chronic nuisance into an acute, time-bounded threat to financial and operational stability.

This report analyzes those forces through five lenses—regulation, technology, cost, competition, and budgeting—to determine where and when urgency peaks for mid-sized utilities, the target segment for emerging “smart water” monitoring start-ups.

### 2.1 Discussion
The most acute forcing functions combine *hard statutory deadlines* and *funding clock expirations.* Notably, the EPA LCRI is the first national rule that explicitly links lead-service-line replacement plans to NRW quantification—effectively making validated leak measurement a compliance prerequisite. Simultaneously, the federal BIL’s digital carve-out pushes utilities to lock contracts by September 2026 or lose grant dollars, creating a procurement sprint well-suited for nimble SaaS/IoT vendors.

*Compiled from AWWA Water Audit Benchmarking Survey 2025, ASCE Infrastructure Report Card 2025, and EPA Safe Drinking Water Information System (SDWIS) 2026 preliminary dataset.*

Key trend observations:

1. Leakage is worsening faster than population growth; pipe break frequency outpaces replacement by 3 : 1 ([ASCE, 2025](https://www.infrastructurereportcard.org)).
2. Inflation-adjusted repair costs have risen 39 % since 2017 due to skilled-labor scarcity and asphalt price spikes.  
3. Regulatory citations for inadequate loss control increased 252 % between 2017 and 2025, reflecting broader state adoption of AWWA M36 validation.

These data demonstrate a **sharply upward trajectory** in both the physical manifestation (breaks) and the financial/legal consequences of leakage.

## 5. Budget & Procurement Timing

1. **Fiscal Year Patterns**  
   • 61 % of U.S. municipalities operate July 1-June 30 FY; 23 % operate Oct 1-Sept 30; remaining follow calendar year ([GFOA, 2024](https://www.gfoa.org/fiscalyear-survey)).  
   • Capital-improvement budgets freeze 4-6 months before FY start.  

2. **Key Windows**  
   • **September-December:** Department heads finalize capital requests; ideal time for vendors to enter.  
   • **January-March:** City councils approve budgets; procurement RFPs drafted.  
   • **April-June:** Solicitations issued, especially for BIL-funded projects lined up for summer construction starts.

3. **Procurement Modalities**  
   • Cooperative purchasing (Sourcewell, HGACBuy) accelerates under $3 M projects.  
   • Engineering service contracts (on-call consultants) often lead selection; technology providers should partner pre-spec.  

4. **Federal Funding Clocks**  
   • BIL SRF dollars must be obligated within 24 months of state capitalization; first tranche (FY 2024) expires **30 Sept 2026**.  
   • Additional IIJA drought-resiliency grants (BOR) expire **31 Dec 2027**.

## 8. Conclusion
External urgency for utilities to tackle leakage is no longer abstract. Concrete deadlines (EPA LCRI 2026, SB 555 2027) align ominously with funding-spend clocks and an exodus of seasoned staff. Cost and risk signals—escalating wholesale water prices, insurance exclusions, and public scorecards—add non-regulatory heat. For mid-sized utilities, the 2025-2028 window represents both a compliance gauntlet and a funding windfall; those without rapid leak-detection solutions face fines, reputational damage, and missed grant opportunities.

Solution providers that can demonstrate quick installation, audit-grade data, and eligibility for SRF/BIL dollars are positioned to ride this “regulatory-funding sandwich.” Timing go-to-market around September-December pre-budget windows, and aligning messaging with EPA LCRI deliverables and SB 555 targets, will maximize traction.
